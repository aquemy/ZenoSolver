The instance, the plans and the Pareto front, as fixed in §1 of the note. Plans are precise objects (routes, journeys, capacity, waiting), and the Lean development transcribes that definition. Definitions are the trusted interface of the whole formalization: every theorem below is about these objects.

Statement (theory note, §1.1).

  • Cities: \(C_I\) (initial) and \(C_G\) (goal), the hubs, and central cities \(1,\dots,n\).
  • Flights are undirected: an existing flight joins two distinct cities and has one duration, used in both directions. \(C_I\)–\(i\) lasts \(d_i \ge 0\), \(i\)–\(C_G\) lasts \(\bar d_i \ge 0\), and a central flight \(i\)–\(j\) (\(i \ne j\)), if it exists, lasts \(d_{ij} = d_{ji} \ge 0\). There is no flight \(C_I\)–\(C_G\). \(\mathrm{dur}(a,b)\) denotes the duration of the flight \(a\)–\(b\).
  • Landing in central city \(i\) costs \(c_i \ge 0\); landing in a hub is free.
  • \(p \ge 1\) planes and \(t \ge p\) passengers start in \(C_I\) at time 0. A plane carries at most one passenger.
  • Durations and times lie in an archimedean ordered field \(\mathbb K\) (\(\mathbb Q\) or \(\mathbb R\)), so that \(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\) is defined. From §7 on, durations are integers.

In Lean. Network holds the durations d, dbar and an arbitrary table dc of central flights; costs are a separate argument c : Fin n → ℕ. Durations, costs and times are natural numbers (format v1); Cert.check_sound_rat extends the checker’s soundness to rational times.

Plans, cost and makespan

Lean Lean definition · Note definition

Statement (theory note, §1.2).

Definition (plan). A plan on a finite set \(Q\) of planes (all \(p\) planes unless stated otherwise) consists of

  1. for every plane \(k \in Q\), a route: a finite sequence of legs \((k,1),\dots,(k,\nu_k)\), where leg \((k,j)\) uses an existing flight joining \(v_{k,j-1}\) and \(v_{k,j}\), flown from \(v_{k,j-1}\) to \(v_{k,j}\), and departs at time \(e_{k,j}\), with \(v_{k,0} = C_I\), \(e_{k,1} \ge 0\) and \(e_{k,j+1} \ge a_{k,j} := e_{k,j} + \mathrm{dur}(v_{k,j-1}, v_{k,j})\);
  2. for every passenger \(\pi\), a journey: a non-empty sequence of legs whose first leg departs from \(C_I\), whose last leg arrives in \(C_G\), and in which each leg departs from the city where the previous one arrives, no earlier than that arrival, and, if both are legs of the same plane, at a later position of its route;
  3. capacity: the journeys are pairwise disjoint, and none repeats a leg.

Below, “flight” also means a leg when no confusion is possible. The plan is feasible if every route ends in \(C_G\) (\(\nu_k \ge 1\) and \(v_{k,\nu_k} = C_G\)). Its cost \(C = \sum_{k,j} c(v_{k,j})\) sums the landing costs of all flights (hubs are free). Its makespan \(M = \max_k a_{k,\nu_k}\) is the time at which the last plane reaches \(C_G\) for the last time. \(\mathcal A\) is the set of pairs \((C, M)\) of feasible plans.

Waiting is implicit: a departure may be later than the preceding arrival, for planes and for passengers. Boarding and debarking are instantaneous: a passenger changing planes debarks when its flight arrives and boards when the next one departs, and the only constraint is the time inequality of item 2. Several planes may be in a city at once.

Sequence order. “First” and “last” refer to positions in a journey or in a route. With zero durations several events can share a time; the sequences fix their order, and every argument below reads them this way.

A plan is event-ordered if its legs have a total order \(\prec\) that extends every route and every journey and along which departure times do not decrease. Lemma 6.8 and Corollary 6.10 use this notion, and Statement 1.2 relates it to PDDL; Theorem 6.9 does not need it. If every flight has a positive duration, every plan is event-ordered: in a route or a journey the next leg departs after the previous one arrives, hence strictly after it departs, so sorting legs by departure time extends both. Plans of the PDDL domain are event-ordered too (Statement 1.2). With zero durations a plan can fail to be: a passenger may ride a later leg of one plane and then an earlier leg of another at the same instant, in a cycle of dependencies that no execution in time realises. Such plans do not change the front (Corollary 6.10).

The flight time of plane \(k\) is \(F_k = \sum_j \mathrm{dur}(f_{k,j})\). Its flights are sequential, so \(F_k \le a_{k,\nu_k} \le M\).

Planes ending anywhere. In the variant of Theorem 4.9, every plan counts (routes need not end in \(C_G\) and may be empty), journeys still end in \(C_G\), and the makespan \(M'\) is the largest arrival time over all legs; there is at least one leg since \(t \ge 1\). \(\mathcal A'\) is the set of pairs \((C, M')\) of all plans.

In Lean. A plan gives every plane a list of legs (Leg: from, to, departure, arrival, passenger). Feasible requires routes chained from C_I at time ≥ 0 and ending in C_G, legs of exact duration, and every passenger’s legs ordered into a route from C_I to C_G; capacity one holds by construction, since a leg names at most one passenger.

Statement 1.2 (PDDL correspondence)

Lean informal · Note sketch

Statement (theory note, §1.3).

Statement 1.2 (PDDL correspondence, S). A plan of the domain under ε-separation yields a plan of §1.2 with the same cost and a makespan at most its total-time: each plane’s fly actions in time order form its route, each person’s flights between board and debark form its journey, and libre gives capacity one; ordering the fly actions by start time (ties by the plan’s order of actions) shows that this plan is event-ordered. Conversely, an event-ordered plan of §1.2 with makespan \(M\) yields PDDL plans with total-time at most \(M + K\varepsilon\), where \(K\) is its number of fly, board and debark actions: list the actions in the event order (each board just before its leg, each debark just after) and delay the \(r\)-th one by \(r\varepsilon\). So the PDDL front tends to the front of event-ordered plans, which is \(\mathcal F\) under (S) and (A1), and under (S) and (AΔ) (the plans of Theorem 4.1 are event-ordered, Lemma 6.8(c); Corollaries 4.8 and 4.12), and under (A1-NS) (Corollary 6.10). The model’s makespan is thus the PDDL makespan in the limit ε → 0, which is what ZenoSolver reports. Whether PDDL planners and VAL accept zero-duration actions is not checked here. This is argued, not formalised.

Statement (theory note, §1.5).

Lemma 1.1 (dominating subset). Let \(\mathcal S' \subseteq \mathcal S \subseteq \mathbb K^2\) with \(\mathcal S'\) finite and every point of \(\mathcal S\) weakly dominated by a point of \(\mathcal S'\). Then \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S = \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S'\), and every point of \(\mathcal S\) is weakly dominated by a point of \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Let \(z \in \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\). Some \(y \in \mathcal S'\) has \(y \preceq z\), so \(y = z\), and \(z\) is minimal in \(\mathcal S' \subseteq \mathcal S\). Let \(z \in \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S'\) and \(y \in \mathcal S\) with \(y \preceq z\). Some \(y' \in \mathcal S'\) has \(y' \preceq y \preceq z\), so \(y' = z\) and then \(y = z\). Definition 2.2 (admissible partition)

Lean Lean definition · Note definition

Statement (theory note, §2).

Definition 2.2 (admissible partition). Let \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) be a partition of \(X\). A parity matrix for it is \(h \in \{0,1\}^{p\times n}\) such that

  • (h1) \(h_{k,i} = 1\) implies \(X_k(i) \ge 1\);
  • (h2) \(h_k := \sum_i h_{k,i} \equiv |X_k| + 1 \pmod 2\) for every plane \(k\);
  • (h3) \(\sum_k h_{k,i} \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) for every city \(i\).

The partition is admissible if it has a parity matrix. Write \(\eta_k = \{i : h_{k,i} = 1\}\), so that \(|\eta_k| = h_k\), and \(Z(h) = \bigcup_k \eta_k\) for the cities that \(h\) touches. A plane with \(|X_k|\) even is an even plane. An empty group would need \(h_k = 0 \equiv 1\) by (h1)–(h2), so admissible partitions have non-empty groups. Admissibility is decidable by enumerating \(h\) on the support of the groups. This definition replaces the component form of the first version (Lemma 2.4 shows they agree), and is the one to formalise.

In Lean. IsParityWitness X h is (h1)–(h3) with h k : Finset (Fin n), one set of cities per plane; Admissible X is ∃ h, IsParityWitness X h, decidable through witnessCandidates.

Statement (theory note, §2).

Lemma 2.6 (link forest) [LF]. A partition is admissible if and only if it has a link forest. An admissible partition has a link forest with at most one edge of each label at each plane.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (If.) Let \(\tilde h_{u,i}\) be the number of edges at \(u\) labelled \(i\). Then \(\tilde h_{u,i} \le X_u(i)\) by (F1), \(\sum_i \tilde h_{u,i} = \deg_\Lambda(u) \equiv |X_u| + 1\) by (F2), and \(\sum_u \tilde h_{u,i}\) is twice the number of edges labelled \(i\). Lemma 2.3 applies. This direction holds for any loopless multigraph (a loop would add 2 to the degree but 1 to \(\tilde h_{u,i}\)).

(Only if.) Take a spanning forest \(\Theta\) of \(H\) and a parity matrix with support \(J \subseteq E(\Theta)\) (Lemma 2.4(b)). At each city \(i\), \(\deg_J(i)\) is even. Split the planes \(J\)-adjacent to \(i\) into \(\deg_J(i)/2\) pairs, and for each pair \(\{u, v\}\) add to \(\Lambda\) an edge \(uv\) labelled \(i\). Then \(u \ne v\), since \(J\) has at most one edge per plane and city. At most one edge at \(u\) is labelled \(i\), and only if \((u,i) \in J\), hence \(X_u(i) \ge 1\): (F1) holds. \(\deg_\Lambda(u) = \deg_J(u) = h_u \equiv |X_u| + 1\): (F2) holds.

\(\Lambda\), built so far as a multigraph, has no cycle, and in particular no parallel edges. Take a cycle: a closed trail \(u_0, u_1, \dots, u_{m-1}, u_0\) with \(m \ge 2\) distinct edges \(u_{j-1}u_j\) labelled \(i_j\) (indices mod \(m\)), with distinct vertices if \(m \ge 3\); \(m = 2\) is the case of parallel edges. It gives the closed walk \(u_0, i_1, u_1, i_2, \dots, i_m, u_0\) in \(J\), which never uses the same edge twice in a row, even cyclically. At a city \(i_j\) it arrives from \(u_{j-1}\) and leaves to \(u_j \ne u_{j-1}\). At a plane \(u_j\) it arrives from \(i_j\) and leaves to \(i_{j+1}\); if \(i_j = i_{j+1}\), then \(u_j\) would belong to two different pairs at that city (the two distinct edges of the cycle at \(u_j\)). But a closed walk of positive length in a forest uses some edge twice in a row: at a vertex of the walk farthest from the root of its tree, both neighbours on the walk are its parent. \(\square\)

Statement (theory note, §4.1).

Theorem 4.1. Assume (S). For every multiset \(X\) with \(|X| = s\) and every admissible partition \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) of \(X\), there is a feasible plan whose central landings are exactly \(X\) (so of cost \(c(X)\)), in which plane \(k\) flies for \(\ell(X_k)\) and picks up at most one passenger, and whose makespan is \(\max_k \ell(X_k)\). The plan uses no central flight, so no assumption on them is needed.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Take a link forest \(\Lambda\) (Lemma 2.6) and root each of its trees at a plane. For a link at \(i\) between a parent \(u\) and a child \(v\), \(u\) will drop a passenger in \(i\) (a segment \(B\)) and \(v\) will pick it up (a segment \(\bar B\)). A non-root plane has exactly one parent link, so every plane picks up at most once. Plane \(u\) uses one copy of \(i\) from \(X_u\) per link at \(u\) labelled \(i\) (F1), and its \(r_u\) free landings, an odd number (F2), for crossings.

Schedule of plane \(k\). Let \(\tau^{\rm dr}_k\) be twice the sum of the durations of its child links (its drop-offs) and \(\tau^{\rm cr}_k\) the load of its free landings.

  1. Its drop-offs, one after the other from time 0: \(C_I \to i \to C_I\), each carrying a new passenger from \(C_I\) and leaving it in \(i\). These never wait.
  2. Its \(r_k\) free landings as crossings \(A, \bar A, A, \dots, A\); each \(A\) carries a new passenger from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\). These never wait.
  3. If \(k\) has a parent link at \(i\): \(C_G \to i \to C_G\), leaving \(i\) once the passenger dropped there by its parent is present, and carrying it to \(C_G\).

Timing. The drop-off of \(u\) at \(i\) reaches \(i\) at a time at most \(\tau^{\rm dr}_u - d_i\). Its child \(v\) reaches \(C_G\) at \(\tau^{\rm dr}_v + \tau^{\rm cr}_v\), arrives in \(i\) at \(\tau^{\rm dr}_v + \tau^{\rm cr}_v + d_i\), leaves at the later of this time and the drop-off’s arrival, and is back at a time at most \(\max(\tau^{\rm dr}_v + \tau^{\rm cr}_v + 2d_i,\ \tau^{\rm dr}_u) = \max(\ell(X_v), \tau^{\rm dr}_u) \le \max(\ell(X_v), \ell(X_u))\). A plane without a parent link finishes at \(\ell(X_k)\). Every plane flies exactly \(\ell(X_k)\), as its segments are simple (Lemma 3.3(c)), so the makespan is \(\max_k \ell(X_k)\).

Passengers. Let \(\Lambda\) have \(N\) links. There are \(\sum_k r_k = s - 2N\) crossings, and each plane makes one more \(A\) than \(\bar A\), so there are \((s - 2N + p)/2 = t - N\) segments \(A\). They and the \(N\) drop-offs carry \(t\) distinct passengers out of \(C_I\), each delivered to \(C_G\) by its \(A\) or by the pick-up linked to its drop-off. Every flight carries at most one passenger, every route ends in \(C_G\), and the central landings are exactly \(X\). \(\square\)

Statement (theory note, §4.2).

Lemma 4.3 (direct selection) [DS]. (a) In a simple plan with clean journeys, whether or not its routes end in \(C_G\), every journey consists of exactly two flights, \(C_I \to x\) and then \(x \to C_G\), for a central city \(x\).

  1. Let \(P\) be a simple plan on a set \(Q\) of planes, every route ending in \(C_G\), with clean journeys and cost \(C\). Then there is a role configuration on \(Q\) with \(t(\mathrm{cfg}) \ge t\), cost at most \(C\), \(\lambda_k \le F_k\) for every \(k\), and \(\mathrm{Dr}_k + \mathrm{Pk}_k + \mathrm{Cr}_k\) a sub-multiset of the central landings of plane \(k\). No assumption on durations is needed.
Proof (theory note)

Proof. (a) The first flight of a journey departs from \(C_I\), so it goes to a central city \(x\). As \(x \ne C_G\), the journey continues with a flight from \(x\), which goes to a hub. That hub is not \(C_I\) (Lemma 3.1), so the flight arrives in \(C_G\), and it is the last flight of the journey (Lemma 3.1).

  1. Journeys. By (a), the first flight of a journey is the first flight of a segment of type \(A\) or \(B\), written \(o(\pi)\), and its second flight is the second flight of a segment at the same city \(x\) of type \(A\) or \(\bar B\), written \(n(\pi)\). A segment has one first flight and one second flight, and by capacity a flight carries at most one passenger, so \(o\) and \(n\) are injective. Passenger \(\pi\) is a hand-off if \(o(\pi) \ne n(\pi)\). A \(B\) is never an \(n(\cdot)\), and a \(\bar B\) never an \(o(\cdot)\).

Counts at a city \(x\). Let \(\beta_x\) be the number of \(B\) segments at \(x\) that are an \(o(\cdot)\), and \(\bar\beta_x\) the number of \(\bar B\) segments at \(x\) that are an \(n(\cdot)\). Among the \(A\) segments at \(x\): \(\theta_x\) are \(o(\pi) = n(\pi)\) for some \(\pi\) (through); \(\omega_x\) are \(o(\pi)\) and \(n(\pi')\) with \(\pi \ne \pi'\) (swaps); \(\alpha_x\) are an \(o(\cdot)\) and no \(n(\cdot)\); \(\bar\alpha_x\) are an \(n(\cdot)\) and no \(o(\cdot)\); the others are empty. Every hand-off at \(x\) has its \(o\) among the \(\beta_x + \alpha_x + \omega_x\) segments counted so, and its \(n\) among the \(\bar\beta_x + \bar\alpha_x + \omega_x\), and each of these segments serves exactly one hand-off at \(x\) in that role. Hence \[\beta_x - \bar\beta_x = \bar\alpha_x - \alpha_x. \tag{1}\] Every passenger has exactly one \(o\), so \[t = \textstyle\sum_x (\theta_x + \omega_x + \alpha_x + \beta_x). \tag{2}\] Let \(z_k\) be the number of non-empty \(A\) segments of plane \(k\). Then \[\textstyle\sum_k z_k = \sum_x (\theta_x + \omega_x + \alpha_x + \bar\alpha_x). \tag{3}\]

Selection. At each city \(x\), let \(\mu_x = \min(\beta_x, \bar\beta_x)\). Select \(\mu_x\) of the \(B\) segments at \(x\) that are an \(o(\cdot)\), and \(\mu_x\) of the \(\bar B\) segments at \(x\) that are an \(n(\cdot)\). For each plane \(k\), let \(z'_k = \max(z_k, 1)\), and select its \(z_k\) non-empty \(A\) segments, \(z'_k - 1\) of its \(\bar A\) segments and, if \(z_k = 0\), one (empty) \(A\) segment. These exist: by Lemma 2.1, plane \(k\) has at least one \(A\), and \(\#\bar A_k = \#A_k - 1 \ge z_k - 1\). Let \(\mathrm{Dr}_k\), \(\mathrm{Pk}_k\) and \(\mathrm{Cr}_k\) be the cities of the selected \(B\), \(\bar B\), and \(A\) or \(\bar A\) segments of \(k\). Then \(|\mathrm{Cr}_k| = 2z'_k - 1\) is odd, and the balance holds since both sides contain \(\mu_x\) copies of \(x\).

Count. By (2) and (3), \(t(\mathrm{cfg}) = \sum_x \mu_x + \sum_k z'_k \ge \sum_x \mu_x + \sum_k z_k = t + \sum_x (\mu_x - \beta_x + \bar\alpha_x)\). By (1), \(\beta_x - \mu_x = (\beta_x - \bar\beta_x)^+ = (\bar\alpha_x - \alpha_x)^+ \le \bar\alpha_x\). So \(t(\mathrm{cfg}) \ge t\).

Loads and cost. The selected segments of \(k\) are distinct and simple, and by Lemma 3.3(c) each lasts exactly its role duration: \(2d_x\) for \(B\), \(2\bar d_x\) for \(\bar B\), \(d_x + \bar d_x\) for \(A\) and \(\bar A\). So \(\lambda_k \le F_k\). Each selected segment contributes its single central landing, so \(\mathrm{Dr}_k + \mathrm{Pk}_k + \mathrm{Cr}_k\) is a sub-multiset of the landings of \(k\), and the cost is at most \(C\). \(\square\)

Statement (theory note, §4.6).

Theorem 4.10 (converse without (A1)). Assume (S) and (AΔ). For every feasible plan with cost \(C\), makespan \(M\) and flight times \(F_k\), there is an admissible partition \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) of a multiset \(X\) with \(|X| = s\), \(c(X) \le C\) and \(\ell(X_k) \le F_k \le M\) for every \(k\). More generally, for a feasible plan on any set \(Q\) of planes with \(t \ge |Q|\), the same holds with \(|Q|\) in place of \(p\): an admissible partition \((X_k)_{k \in Q}\) with \(|X| = 2t - |Q|\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. We write the proof for all \(p\) planes. It uses \(t \ge p\) only for Lemma 4.11, through \(s \ge p\), so it holds word for word for any set \(Q\) with \(t \ge |Q|\). Clean the plan (Lemma 3.1); this keeps the routes. For a segment \(\gamma\), let \(R(\gamma)\) be the largest \(d_x\) over its central landings \(x\). By Lemma 3.3(b) and (S), \(F(\gamma) \ge 2R(\gamma)\). Plane \(k\) has \(\sigma_k \ge 1\) segments, and \(\sigma = \sum_k \sigma_k \ge s\) (Lemma 3.2).

A selection is a set of segments, at least one per plane, with for each chosen \(\gamma\) a city \(x(\gamma)\) such that \(d_{x(\gamma)} \le R(\gamma)\), and a central landing \(\pi(\gamma)\) of the plan in \(x(\gamma)\), with \(\pi\) injective. Its groups \(X_k = \{x(\gamma) : \gamma \text{ chosen, of plane } k\}\) satisfy \(\ell(X_k) \le \sum_{\gamma \text{ chosen}} 2R(\gamma) \le \sum_{\gamma} F(\gamma) = F_k \le M\), the last sum running over all segments of \(k\), and \(c(X) = \sum_\gamma c(\pi(\gamma)) \le C\). We build a selection whose groups are admissible with \(|X| \ge s\) and \(|X| \equiv s \pmod 2\). Lemma 4.11 then removes landings two at a time, down to \(|X| = s\), keeping the groups admissible and making them smaller.

Step 1: sharing components. Two planes share a central city if both land in it. Hubs do not count: every plane lands in \(C_G\). Let \(K\) be a connected component of the graph on planes whose edges join planes that share a central city. Let \(q_K\) be its number of planes, \(\sigma_K\) its number of segments, and \(t_K\) the number of passengers whose journey uses a plane of \(K\). A clean journey arrives in a hub only with its last flight. So two consecutive flights of a journey on different planes meet in a central city. The first plane lands there, and so does the second, whose route starts in \(C_I\). Every journey therefore stays inside one component, \(\sum_K t_K = t\), and \(K\) with its passengers is a feasible plan. Lemma 3.2, whose proof does not use \(t \ge p\), gives \(\sigma_K \ge 2t_K - q_K\), even when \(t_K < q_K\); write \(\delta_K = \sigma_K - 2t_K + q_K \ge 0\), so that \(\sum_K \delta_K = \sigma - s\). The number of planes of \(K\) with \(\sigma_k\) even has the parity of \(q_K - \sigma_K\), hence of \(\delta_K\). If \(\delta_K\) is odd, some plane \(\kappa_K\) of \(K\) has \(\sigma_{\kappa_K} \ge 2\); otherwise \(\sigma_K = q_K\) and \(\delta_K = 2(q_K - t_K)\) would be even. Let \(\mathcal T\) be the set of planes with \(\sigma_k\) even, with membership of \(\kappa_K\) flipped for every \(K\) with \(\delta_K\) odd. Every component then contains an even number of planes of \(\mathcal T\). Plane \(\kappa_K\) will leave out one segment, so that \(\mathcal T\) is the set of planes with an even number of chosen segments.

Step 2: links. A link \((\gamma, \gamma', y)\) joins segments \(\gamma, \gamma'\) of two different planes at a city \(y\) with \(d_y \le \min(R(\gamma), R(\gamma'))\). Its ends are \((\gamma, y)\) and \((\gamma', y)\). Each end will carry a payment, a central landing in \(y\), and no landing will pay for two ends. The degree of a plane is the number of ends at its segments. Take a spanning forest of the sharing graph, and in it the \(\mathcal T\)-join \(J\) built as in the proof of Lemma 2.4(b): the edge from a vertex to its parent is in \(J\) iff the vertex’s subtree contains an odd number of planes of \(\mathcal T\). Every tree has an even number of them, so in \(J\) the planes of \(\mathcal T\) have odd degree and the others even degree. For each edge \(\{u, w\}\) of \(J\), pick a central city that \(u\) and \(w\) share, and segments of \(u\) and of \(w\) that land in it, and make a link there. While a segment \(\gamma\) has two ends at one city \(y\), in links \((\gamma, \gamma_1, y)\) and \((\gamma, \gamma_2, y)\), replace them by \((\gamma_1, \gamma_2, y)\), or delete both if \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) belong to the same plane. This keeps every plane’s degree mod 2 and lowers the number of links. At this point every end is at a city in which its segment lands, and no segment has two ends at one city. Pay each end with a landing of its own segment in its city. Later steps move ends to cities their segment does not visit. Only the payments and the bound by \(R\) are tracked from now on.

Re-pairing. Let a segment \(\gamma\) have ends in the links \(\lambda_1 = (\gamma, \gamma_1, y_1)\) and \(\lambda_2 = (\gamma, \gamma_2, y_2)\), with \(d_{y_1} \le d_{y_2}\). Re-pairing them replaces both links by \((\gamma_1, \gamma_2, y_1)\). In it, \(\gamma_1\) keeps its payment from \(\lambda_1\), and \(\gamma_2\) receives the payment of \(\gamma\) in \(\lambda_1\). The payments of \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma_2\) in \(\lambda_2\) are released. If \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) belong to the same plane, both links are deleted instead and all their payments released. A re-pairing keeps the following properties:

  • every end is within \(R\), as \(d_{y_1} \le d_{y_2} \le R(\gamma_2)\);
  • payments are in the right city and exclusive;
  • every plane’s degree mod 2: the plane of \(\gamma\) loses two ends, and the planes of \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) keep one end each, or their common plane loses two;
  • no segment gains an end.

It also lowers the number of links.

Step 3: one end per segment. For every segment \(\gamma\) fix one of its landings \(z(\gamma)\) in a city of duration \(R(\gamma)\). While some segment \(\gamma\) has \(r \ge 2\) ends, sort them by the duration of their city, putting last the end paid by \(z(\gamma)\) if there is one. This is possible, because every end of \(\gamma\) is within \(R(\gamma)\). Then re-pair the first end with the second, the third with the fourth, and so on. Afterwards \(\gamma\) has at most one end, and never gains another. The loop ends because the number of links decreases.

Claim: no landing \(z(\gamma)\) ever pays for an end of another segment. Payments are only ever moved from one end to another, or released, and a released landing never pays an end again. So a landing of \(\gamma\) can pay for another segment only if \(\gamma\) hands it over, which happens only when \(\gamma\) is the middle segment of a re-pairing. That happens only when \(\gamma\) itself is processed, which is at most once, and only for its ends at odd positions. If \(z(\gamma)\) pays an end of \(\gamma\), that end is at position \(r\): for \(r\) even its payment is released, and for \(r\) odd it is not re-paired.

Step 4: the left-out segments. For each \(K\) with \(\delta_K\) odd, consider \(\kappa = \kappa_K\). If \(\kappa\) has a segment without an end, leave it out. Otherwise two segments \(\gamma, \gamma'\) of \(\kappa\) have one end each, in links \((\gamma, \gamma_1, y)\) and \((\gamma', \gamma_2, y')\) with \(d_y \le d_{y'}\). Replace both by \((\gamma_1, \gamma_2, y)\), or delete both if \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) belong to the same plane. Here \(\gamma_1\) keeps its payment and \(\gamma_2\) receives the payment of \(\gamma\); the payments of \(\gamma'\) and of \(\gamma_2\) at \(y'\) are released. The four properties hold as for a re-pairing, with \(\kappa\) losing two ends. Leave out \(\gamma\): its landing may now pay for \(\gamma_2\), but \(\gamma\) will not be chosen. The segment \(\gamma'\) has no end left and handed nothing over. So the claim still holds for every segment that is not left out.

Step 5: the selection. Choose all segments that are not left out. A chosen segment with an end takes the city of its end and the end’s payment. One without an end takes the city of \(z(\gamma)\) and \(z(\gamma)\), which by the claim pays for no end. This \(\pi\) is injective, and every city is within \(R\). Plane \(k\) has \(m_k = \sigma_k\) chosen segments, or \(\sigma_k - 1\) if \(k\) is some \(\kappa_K\). So \(m_k \ge 1\), and \(m_k\) is even exactly when \(k \in \mathcal T\). Let \(\tilde h_{k,y}\) be the number of chosen segments of \(k\) with an end at \(y\). Then \(\tilde h_{k,y} \le X_k(y)\). The row sum \(\sum_y \tilde h_{k,y}\) is the degree of \(k\), which is odd iff \(k \in \mathcal T\); hence it is \(\equiv m_k + 1 = |X_k| + 1\). The column sum \(\sum_k \tilde h_{k,y}\) is twice the number of links at \(y\). By Lemma 2.3 the groups are admissible. Their size is \(\sigma - \#\{K : \delta_K \text{ odd}\}\), which is at least \(\sigma - \sum_K \delta_K = s\) and has the parity of \(s\). \(\square\)

Lemma 6.3 (template)

Statement (theory note, §6.2).

Lemma 6.3 (template) [N3]. The template is a schedule, and its makespan is \[M_{\rm tmpl} = \max\Big(\max_k \lambda_k,\ \max_{(u,j;\,v,l) \in \mathcal L \text{ at } i}\ \mathrm{Pre}_u(j) + d_i + \bar d_i + \mathrm{Suf}_v(l)\Big).\] Every schedule of the same configuration, orders and matching has makespan at least \(M_{\rm tmpl}\). Proof (theory note)

Proof. Lower bound. In any such schedule, plane \(u\) starts its \(j\)-th drop-off after flying its earlier drop-offs, so this drop-off reaches \(i\) no earlier than \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) + d_i\). The linked pick-up of \(v\) leaves \(i\) no earlier, flies \(\bar d_i\) to \(C_G\), and \(v\) then still flies its later pick-ups, at least \(\mathrm{Suf}_v(l)\). Also every plane flies \(\lambda_k\) (Lemma 3.3(c)), so it finishes no earlier than \(\lambda_k\).

Template. Drop-offs come first and wait for nothing, so the \(j\)-th drop-off of \(u\) reaches \(i\) exactly at \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) + d_i\), and no wait is cyclic. Let \(\phi_v(l)\) be the time \(v\) returns to \(C_G\) after its \(l\)-th pick-up, with \(\phi_v(0) = 2\delta(\mathrm{Dr}_v) + \delta(\mathrm{Cr}_v) + \bar\delta(\mathrm{Cr}_v)\). Unrolling, the last return of \(v\) is the maximum of \(\lambda_v\) and of the terms of the formula for the links into \(v\). Planes without pick-ups finish at \(\lambda_k\). A shuttle triple at \((x, y)\) is a drop-off at \(x\) (a segment \(B\)), a shuttle from \(x\) to \(y\), and a pick-up at \(y\) (a segment \(\bar B\)), the three carrying one passenger. This is the \(B\bar A\bar B\) situation of the arXiv paper.
  • A split crossing at \((x, y)\) is a segment \(C_I \to x \to y \to C_G\) (type \(A\)) whose central flight is empty: its first flight leaves a passenger in \(x\), and its last flight carries another passenger from \(y\) to \(C_G\). It acts as a drop-off at \(x\) and a pick-up at \(y\).
Remarks in the note

A shuttle from \(y\) to \(x\) and a split crossing at \((x, y)\) fly the same three flights in opposite directions. Both last \(d_x + d_{xy} + \bar d_y\), and both take one passenger out of \(y\) and leave one in \(x\). But a split crossing is a crossing, while a shuttle is a return segment, whose plane needs two more crossings (Lemma 2.1). By Lemma 3.3(b), under (AΔ), both last at least \(\max(d_x + \bar d_x,\ d_y + \bar d_y)\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. The only triangles are \(C_I, 1, 2\) and \(C_G, 1, 2\). In both, \(d_{12} = 2\) is at most 4, the sum of the other two durations, and at least 2, their difference. So (AΔ) holds, and (A1-NS) fails as \(2 < \min(d_1 + d_2,\ \bar d_1 + \bar d_2) = 4\).

A plan of makespan 8. Plane 1 flies \(C_I \to 1 \to C_I\), leaving \(\pi_1\) in 1 at time 1. It then carries \(\pi_2\) along \(C_I \to 2 \to C_G\), from time 2 to 6. Finally it flies \(C_G \to 2 \to C_G\), picking up \(\pi_3\) in 2 at time 7 and arriving at 8. Plane 2 flies the split crossing \(C_I \to 2 \to 1 \to C_G\). It leaves \(\pi_3\) in 2 at time 3, flies empty to 1, arriving at 5, and carries \(\pi_1\) to \(C_G\), arriving at 8. The landings are 1, 2, 2 and 2, 1, so the cost is \(2c_1 + 3c_2\).

Lower bound. By Lemma 3.3(b), a segment of type \(A\) or \(\bar A\) lasts at least \(\min_x (d_x + \bar d_x) = 4\). A plane with a segment \(\bar A\) has at least two segments \(A\) (Lemma 2.1), so it flies at least 12. A plan of makespan below 12 whose central flights lie in segments \(\bar A\) therefore has no central flight. It is a plan of the instance without the central flight, which satisfies (A1-NS), and Theorem 6.1 gives a role configuration with passenger count 3 and every \(\lambda_k \le M\). Crossings last 4 in both cities. A drop-off lasts 2 in city 1 and 6 in city 2, and a pick-up 6 in city 1 and 2 in city 2. The configuration has \(s = 4\) landings and every \(|\mathrm{Cr}_k|\) odd. So either a plane has three crossings, with load at least 12, or each plane has one crossing and there is one link, a drop-off and a pick-up at one city \(x\). A plane with both has load at least \(4 + 2d_x + 2\bar d_x = 12\). Otherwise the two loads are \(4 + 2d_x\) and \(4 + 2\bar d_x\), and \(\max(d_x, \bar d_x) = 3\) in both cities. So \(M \ge 10\). Mechanism. The split crossing of Proposition 6.7 flies against the grain. It enters at city 2, next to \(C_G\) (\(\bar d_2 = 1\)), and leaves from city 1, next to \(C_I\) (\(d_1 = 1\)). Lemma 6.19 (cut bound)

Statement (theory note, §6.4).

Lemma 6.19 (cut bound). Let \(\Phi\) be the least flight time of a sequence of flights from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\). For every feasible plan, \(\sum_k F_k \ge (2t - p)\,\Phi\), so its makespan is at least \((2t - p)\Phi/p\). No assumption is needed. Under (AΔ), \(\Phi = \min_x (d_x + \bar d_x)\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Let \(\phi(v)\) be the least flight time of a sequence of flights from \(C_I\) to the city \(v\), so \(\phi(C_I) = 0\), \(\phi(C_G) = \Phi\), and \(|\phi(a) - \phi(b)| \le \mathrm{dur}(a, b)\) for every flight. For a leg \(\ell\) from \(a\) to \(b\) let \(\Delta(\ell) = \phi(b) - \phi(a)\). Along the route of plane \(k\) the \(\Delta\) sum to \(\Phi\). So its upward part \(U_k = \sum_\ell \Delta(\ell)^+\) and its downward part \(D_k = \sum_\ell \Delta(\ell)^-\) satisfy \(U_k - D_k = \Phi\), and \(F_k \ge U_k + D_k = 2U_k - \Phi\). Along the journey of a passenger the \(\Delta\) also sum to \(\Phi\), so the upward parts of its legs add up to at least \(\Phi\). Journeys are disjoint (capacity), so \(\sum_k U_k \ge t\Phi\) and \(\sum_k F_k \ge 2t\Phi - p\Phi\). Every \(F_k\) is at most the makespan. Under (AΔ), take a sequence of flights from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\), its first arrival in \(C_G\), and its last visit to \(C_I\) before that. Between them it visits only central cities, and if \(x\) is the last of them, that part lasts at least \(d_x + \bar d_x\) by the induction of Lemma 3.3(b). The sequence \(C_I \to x \to C_G\) attains it. \(\square\)

Lemma 6.8 (event order). The next-leg relation of a plan relates a leg to the next leg on its route and to the next leg of the journey it belongs to, if any.

  1. A plan is event-ordered iff its next-leg relation has no cycle.

  2. In a simple plan with clean journeys, a cycle of the next-leg relation passes through a central city \(x\) with \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\).

  3. The template of a role configuration (§6.2), and the plan of Theorem 4.1, are event-ordered.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (a) An event order extends the relation, so the relation has no cycle. Conversely, along the relation departure times do not decrease, since the next leg departs no earlier than the previous one arrives; sorting the legs by departure time, ties broken by a topological order of the acyclic relation, gives an event order.

  1. Along each step the next leg departs no earlier than the previous one departs plus its duration, so every leg on a cycle has zero duration. Steps within one plane go forward on its route (§1.2, item 2), so a cycle contains a journey step between two planes. By Lemma 4.3(a) that step goes from a leg \(C_I \to x\) to a leg \(x \to C_G\), both on the cycle, so \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\).

  2. Give the legs of a plane’s drop-offs phase 1, of its crossings phase 2 and of its pick-ups phase 3. A step of the relation either stays on one plane and goes forward on its route, or follows a link from the first leg of a drop-off (phase 1) to the second leg of a pick-up (phase 3). Phases never decrease along the relation, and they increase at every step between two planes. A cycle therefore has no such step and stays on one route, which is impossible. The plan of Theorem 4.1 has the same three phases. \(\square\)

Theorem 6.9 (schedulable domination)

Lean machine-checked · Note proof in the note · Hypotheses (A1-NS) · Uses 2.1, 3.1, 3.3, 4.3(a), 4.4, 6.1, 6.3

Statement (theory note, §6.2).

Theorem 6.9 (schedulable domination) [SD]. Assume (A1-NS). Let a feasible plan have cost \(C\) and makespan \(M\). Then there are a role configuration on all \(p\) planes with passenger count \(t\) and cost at most \(C\), orders of its drop-offs and pick-ups, and a link matching whose template has makespan at most \(M\). Neither an event order, nor (AΔ), nor (AΔ-path) is needed.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Step 1: a simple plan with clean journeys. Replace central flights and clean, as in the proof of Theorem 6.1; cost, arrival times, flight times and makespan do not increase. By Lemma 4.3(a), every journey is now a leg \(\alpha_\pi : C_I \to x\) followed by a leg \(\beta_\pi : x \to C_G\), which departs no earlier than \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives. Every route is a sequence of segments, one per landing. For a segment \(\gamma\) at a city \(x\), let \(a(\gamma)\) be the time its first leg arrives in \(x\) and \(e(\gamma)\) the time its second leg departs, so \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma)\). From here on only the routes and the pairs \((\alpha_\pi, \beta_\pi)\) matter: the moves below keep the routes valid, but need not keep a plan in the sense of item 2 of §1.2.

Step 2: rewiring. Next to the routes, keep a set of loops, empty at first: closed walks whose legs all depart and arrive at one time \(\tau\), so last \(0\), and alternate between hubs and central cities, so that a loop is made of segments too. A passenger is carried through if \(\alpha_\pi\) and \(\beta_\pi\) are the two legs of one segment, of a route or of a loop. Otherwise it is a hand-off, from the segment \(\gamma\) of \(\alpha_\pi\) to the segment \(\gamma'\) of \(\beta_\pi\), both at its city \(x\). While one of these moves applies, apply it.

  • Exchange. \(\gamma\) is on the route of \(u\), \(\gamma'\) on the route of \(v \ne u\), and \(a(\gamma') \le e(\gamma)\): \(v\) is in \(x\) before \(u\) leaves. Give \(u\) the legs of \(v\) from \(\beta_\pi\) on, and \(v\) the legs of \(u\) after \(\alpha_\pi\). Both routes stay valid: \(\beta_\pi\) departs after \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives, and the second leg of \(\gamma\) departs after the first leg of \(\gamma'\) arrives.
  • Cut. \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma'\) are on one route, \(\gamma'\) before \(\gamma\). The route gives \(e(\gamma') \le a(\gamma)\) and the journey \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma')\), so every leg from \(\beta_\pi\) to \(\alpha_\pi\) departs and arrives at \(\tau = e(\gamma')\) and lasts \(0\); in particular \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\). These legs form a closed walk at \(x\). Remove it from the route, which keeps the first leg of \(\gamma'\) and the second leg of \(\gamma\), a segment at \(x\) since \(a(\gamma') \le \tau \le e(\gamma)\). The walk becomes a loop; its segments are \((\alpha_\pi, \beta_\pi)\) and the segments strictly between \(\gamma'\) and \(\gamma\).
  • Splice. One of \(\gamma, \gamma'\) is a segment \(F\) of a loop at time \(\tau\), the other a segment \(\delta\) of a route, and \(e(\gamma) > a(\gamma')\). Then \(a(\delta) < \tau \le e(\delta)\) or \(a(\delta) \le \tau < e(\delta)\): if \(F = \gamma\), then \(e(\gamma) = \tau > a(\delta)\) and \(\tau \le e(\delta)\) by the journey; if \(F = \gamma'\), then \(a(\delta) \le \tau\) by the journey and \(e(\delta) > \tau\). Insert the loop into the route at \(\delta\): the first leg of \(\delta\), the second leg of \(F\), the loop around to \(F\), the first leg of \(F\), the second leg of \(\delta\). The route stays valid, and \(\pi\) is carried through by the new segment of \(\alpha_\pi\) and \(\beta_\pi\).

Every move keeps the legs, so the cost, and every route’s last leg or exchanges two of them, so the makespan. It keeps the routes valid and the loops closed walks at one time, and it carries one more passenger through. It carries none less: the only segments it breaks are \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma'\), and a segment carrying another passenger through holds both of that passenger’s legs, so it is neither. So the moves stop, after at most \(t\) of them. Then every hand-off from \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma'\) has \(e(\gamma) \le a(\gamma')\):

  • between the routes of two planes, the exchange does not apply, so \(e(\gamma) < a(\gamma')\);
  • on one route, the cut does not apply, so \(\gamma'\) comes after \(\gamma\), and \(e(\gamma) \le a(\gamma')\) along the route;
  • between a loop and a route, the splice does not apply;
  • between two loops, at times \(\tau\) and \(\tau'\): \(e(\gamma) = \tau\) is when \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives and \(a(\gamma') = \tau'\) when \(\beta_\pi\) departs.

Step 3: paths. At each city \(x\), draw an arrow from \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma'\) for each hand-off at \(x\); the segments are those of the routes and of the loops. Each leg carries at most one passenger, so every segment has at most one outgoing and one incoming arrow. A \(B\) has no incoming arrow, since its second leg returns to \(C_I\), which clean journeys never reach, and a \(\bar B\) has no outgoing arrow. So the arrows form disjoint paths \(\gamma_0 \to \gamma_1 \to \dots \to \gamma_m\) with \(m \ge 1\), and cycles. The inner segments of a path, and all segments of a cycle, have an incoming and an outgoing arrow, so they are \(A\) segments, each dropping one passenger and picking up another. Along a path, by step 2, \(a(\gamma_0) \le e(\gamma_0) \le a(\gamma_1) \le \dots \le e(\gamma_m)\).

Step 4: selection. Attach each loop to any plane. Take every \(A\) and \(\bar A\) segment of each plane, on its route and its loops, as a crossing. A route from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) has \(\#A - \#\bar A = 1\) (Lemma 2.1) and a closed loop \(0\), so each plane gets an odd number of crossings. For each path from a \(B\) to a \(\bar B\), take its \(B\) as a drop-off and its \(\bar B\) as a pick-up, linked to each other. Take no other segment. The balance holds link by link. A passenger carried through rides an \(A\), which carries no arrow; every other passenger is an arrow of exactly one path or cycle. A cycle of \(k\) arrows consists of \(k\) \(A\) segments. A path with \(m\) arrows contributes its \(m - 1\) inner \(A\) segments, one more for each end that is an \(A\), and one link if it runs from a \(B\) to a \(\bar B\): at least \(m\) in every case. So the passenger count, the number of links plus the number of \(A\) segments, is at least \(t\). Distinct segments have distinct first legs, each a leg of the plan of step 1 landing at the segment’s city, so the selected segments are distinct landings of that plan and the cost is at most \(C\). A route segment is simple and lasts its role duration (Lemma 3.3(c)); a loop segment has role duration \(0\), since its legs last \(0\). So \(\lambda_k \le F_k \le M\), where \(F_k\) is the flight time of the rewired route of plane \(k\): it is valid, starts at time \(\ge 0\) and ends by \(M\).

Step 5: the closed form. The closed form \(M_{\rm tmpl}\) of Lemma 6.3 makes sense for any configuration, orders and matching. Order the drop-offs of each plane with those of its loops first, then those of its route in route order, and its pick-ups with those of its route in route order, then those of its loops. Use the links of step 4. Take a link from the \(j\)-th drop-off \(\gamma\) of \(u\) to the \(l\)-th pick-up \(\gamma'\) of \(v\), at \(x\). If \(\gamma\) is on the route, \(u\) flies its earlier route drop-offs before \(\gamma\), and the loop drop-offs weigh \(0\), so \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) + d_x \le a(\gamma)\); if \(\gamma\) is in a loop, \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) = d_x = 0\). If \(\gamma'\) is on the route, \(v\) flies \(\bar d_x\) to \(C_G\) after \(e(\gamma')\) and then its later route pick-ups, all by time \(M\), and the loop pick-ups weigh \(0\), so \(e(\gamma') + \bar d_x + \mathrm{Suf}_v(l) \le M\); if \(\gamma'\) is in a loop at time \(\tau\), \(\bar d_x = \mathrm{Suf}_v(l) = 0\) and \(e(\gamma') = \tau \le M\): every leg of a loop was a leg of a route before that route’s last leg, which arrives by \(M\). As \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma')\) (step 3), the term of the link is at most \(M\). Together with \(\lambda_k \le M\), \(M_{\rm tmpl} \le M\).

Step 6: shrink. Lemma 4.4 (\(t \ge p\), §1.1) brings the passenger count to \(t\), deleting two crossings of a plane or a drop-off together with a pick-up at its city. Take the pick-up linked to it, so that the remaining links still match drop-offs and pick-ups city by city. With the same orders, deleting crossings only lowers loads, and deleting a link lowers loads and the \(\mathrm{Pre}\) and \(\mathrm{Suf}\) of the other links, so \(M_{\rm tmpl}\) stays at most \(M\). The configuration now has passenger count \(t\), and by Lemma 6.3 its template has makespan \(M_{\rm tmpl} \le M\). \(\square\)

Remarks in the note

The previous version of this proof kept a feasible, event-ordered plan through step 2. It exchanged only the hand-offs that overlap in the event order, so it needed one, and it left open plans that are not event-ordered on instances with a city of \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\). General flight graphs

An instance on any flight graph can be reduced to a clique instance, one central city per path (§6.6 of the note). The reduction does not keep the front in general: Example 6.11 is a counterexample. Propositions 6.12–6.16 say what the reduction does give, where it is exact, and how to compute the graph’s front directly.

Example 6.11 (the reduction misses the front)

Lean informal · Note refuted · Uses 6.3, 6.10, 6.13

Statement (theory note, §6.6).

Example 6.11 (the reduction misses the front). Let \(U\) be the path \(C_I\)–\(a\)–\(b\)–\(C_G\) with durations \(1, 2, 1\) and \(c_a = c_b = 1\), with \(t = 3\) and \(p = 2\). Then \(f(U)\) has two cities, \(v_a = (C_I a,\ a b C_G)\) with \((d, \bar d, c) = (1, 3, 2)\) and \(v_b = (C_I a b,\ b C_G)\) with \((3, 1, 2)\), and \[\mathcal F_{f(U)} = \{(8, 10)\}, \qquad \mathcal F_U = \{(8, 8)\}.\]

Proof (theory note)

Proof. The reduced front. \(f(U)\) has no central flights, so Corollary 6.10 applies. A role configuration with passenger count 3 on 2 planes has \(s = 4\) landings (Definition 4.2), each of cost 2, so it costs 8. Either one plane has three crossings, and a load of at least 12, or each plane has one crossing (load 4) and there is one link, a drop-off and a pick-up at the same city. These weigh 2 and 6 at \(v_a\), and 6 and 2 at \(v_b\), so some plane has a load of at least 10. By Lemma 6.3 every least makespan is at least 10. The template of a link at \(v_a\), with the drop-off on one plane and the pick-up on the other, reaches 10: the pick-up plane crosses in \([0, 4]\), reaches \(v_a\) at 7, after the drop-off’s arrival at 1, and is back at 10. So \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal K = \{(8, 10)\}\).

The graph. Every plan costs at least \(s\kappa^\star = 4 \cdot 2\) (Proposition 6.13). Each edge of the path separates \(C_I\) from \(C_G\). Every passenger flies across it towards \(C_G\) on some leg, and a leg carries at most one passenger, so the planes fly it towards \(C_G\) at least \(t = 3\) times. Each plane ends on the \(C_G\) side, so it flies the edge towards \(C_G\) once more than towards \(C_I\), and the planes fly it towards \(C_I\) at least \(t - p = 1\) time. They thus fly at least \(4 \cdot (1 + 2 + 1) = 16\) in total, one of them at least 8. The following plan costs 8 and has makespan 8 (legs as from → to [departure, arrival]):

  • plane 1: \(C_I \to a\) [0, 1], \(a \to b\) [1, 3], \(b \to a\) [3, 5], \(a \to b\) [5, 7], \(b \to C_G\) [7, 8];
  • plane 2: \(C_I \to a\) [0, 1], \(a \to C_I\) [1, 2], \(C_I \to a\) [2, 3], \(a \to b\) [3, 5], \(b \to C_G\) [5, 6], \(C_G \to b\) [6, 7], \(b \to C_G\) [7, 8];
  • passenger 1 flies plane 2 from \(C_I\) at 2 to \(C_G\); passenger 2 flies plane 1 to \(b\), arriving at 3, and plane 2 from \(b\) at 7; passenger 3 flies plane 2 to \(a\), arriving at 1, and plane 1 from \(a\) at 5 to \(C_G\).

Each plane lands twice in \(a\) and twice in \(b\), and each passenger changes planes at most once. \(\square\)

Remarks in the note

The bound shows what \(f(U)\) cannot do. Beyond one crossing per plane, the planes must fly one more round trip on each edge, \(2 + 4 + 2\) in all. A link of \(f(U)\) gives the round trip on \(C_I\)–\(a\) to one plane and the two others to the other plane (\(v_a\)), or splits after \(b\) (\(v_b\)). The plan above gives \(a\)–\(b\) to plane 1 and the two outer edges to plane 2, \(4 + 4\).

Plane 1 does this in one segment, \(C_I a b a b C_G\): it drops passenger 2 at \(b\) at time 3, flies the central edge \(b \to a\), and picks passenger 3 up at \(a\) at time 5. That is what \(f(U)\) lacks. Its cities have no central flights (\(d_{ij} = \infty\)), and through a hub a plane flies \(\bar d_{v_b} + \bar d_{v_a} = 4\), or \(d_{v_b} + d_{v_a} = 4\), between \(v_b\) and \(v_a\): twice the edge \(a\)–\(b\). This is the short-central-flight mechanism of §6.4, and the reason Proposition 6.15(a) needs (A1-NS). Relaying passengers is not the issue: in the plan above, as in every plan of \(f(U)\) after cleaning (Lemma 4.3(a)), each passenger changes planes at most once. The same routes also let passenger 3 be relayed along three legs of two planes, but that is not needed.

Giving \(f(U)\) its central flights back does not repair the reduction either. Add a central flight of duration 2 between \(v_a\) and \(v_b\). Plane 1’s segment then becomes \(C_I \to v_b \to v_a \to C_G\), but plane 2’s drop-off at \(v_a\) and pick-up at \(v_b\) are no longer paired at one city, and \(f\) charges each of them 2 instead of 1 (Proposition 6.12, cost). The front of this instance is \(\{(8, 10), (10, 8)\}\) (computed, §11).

Bucket brigade. On the same path with durations \(1, 1, 1\), \(t = 4\) and \(p = 3\), the same arguments give \(\mathcal F_{f(U)} = \{(10, 7)\}\) and \(\mathcal F_U = \{(10, 5)\}\). In a plan of makespan 5 each plane flies exactly one edge back and forth, a different edge per plane, so one route is \(C_I a b a b C_G\), the segment of Example 6.11. With the routes \(C_I a C_I a b C_G\), \(C_I a b a b C_G\) and \(C_I a b C_G b C_G\), the passengers can be assigned so that each changes planes at most once (checks/general_graph.py). The same routes also allow a relay through all three planes, one edge each, but it is not needed. With central flights back, \(f(U)\) gives \(\{(10, 7), (12, 5)\}\).

Inner points. The reduction also misses points inside the front. Take the spokes \(C_I\)–1–2–\(C_G\), with durations \(1, 2, 3\), and \(C_I\)–3–4–\(C_G\), with durations \(1, 1, 1\), costs \(c = (3, 4, 5, 5)\), \(t = 3\) and \(p = 2\). The exact front is \((40, 7), (37, 8), (34, 9), (31, 11), (28, 12)\), and \(f(U)\) misses \((31, 11)\). The plan the flow model finds for that point has the route \(C_I\,1\,2\,1\,2\,C_G\), the segment of Example 6.11 on the first spoke (§11). On random tiny graphs the two fronts differ on 342 of 4,000 paths, spokes, cycles and random graphs, 22% of those with \(p \ge 2\) and \(t > p\), and on none of 1,000 clique instances under (A1-NS) (§11).

Proposition 6.12 (the reduced front is attained)

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Uses 6.3, 6.10

Statement (theory note, §6.6).

Proposition 6.12 (the reduced front is attained). For every role configuration of \(f(U)\) with passenger count \(t\), every order of its drop-offs and pick-ups and every link matching, \(U\) has a feasible plan with the configuration’s cost and the makespan \(M_{\rm tmpl}\) of its template (Lemma 6.3). Hence \(\mathcal F_{f(U)} \subseteq \mathcal A_U\). No assumption on \(U\) is needed.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Fly the template in \(U\), with each leg between a hub and a city \(v = (w, e)\) replaced by the path it stands for: \(C_I \to v\) by \(w\), \(v \to C_G\) by \(e\), and the opposite legs by the reversed paths. Each path is flown without waiting from the departure of the template leg, so it reaches its last vertex when that leg arrives, and every route ends in \(C_G\) when the template’s does. The passenger of a crossing stays on board through its segment. The passenger of a link from a drop-off to a pick-up at \(v = (w, e)\) flies the drop-off along \(w\) to \(i\), waits there, and flies the pick-up along \(e\) to \(C_G\): the template has the pick-up leave \(v\) no earlier than the drop-off arrives, and the paths keep these times. Every leg of \(U\) carries at most the passenger of the template leg it belongs to. So the plan is feasible, with makespan \(M_{\rm tmpl}\).

Cost. Let \(w = (C_I = u_0, \dots, u_a = i)\) and \(e = (i = x_0, \dots, x_b = C_G)\). The landings in \(U\) of a segment at \(v\) cost:

  • \(\omega(w) + \omega(e) = c_v\) for a crossing \(A\);
  • \((\omega(e) + c_i) + (\omega(w) - c_i) = c_v\) for a crossing \(\bar A\), since \(e\) reversed lands in \(x_{b-1}, \dots, x_0\) and \(w\) reversed in \(u_{a-1}, \dots, u_0\);
  • \(2\omega(w) - c_i\) for a drop-off and \(2\omega(e) + c_i\) for a pick-up.

So \(f\) charges a single drop-off or pick-up wrongly, but a drop-off and a pick-up at the same city cost \(2\omega(w) + 2\omega(e) = 2c_v\) together, as \(f\) charges them. By the balance, the link matching pairs the drop-offs and the pick-ups of every city, so the plan costs what the configuration costs. By Corollary 6.10, whose (A1-NS) holds as \(f(U)\) has no central flights, every point of \(\mathcal F_{f(U)}\) is the cost and least makespan of such a configuration. \(\square\)

Proposition 6.13 (same least cost)

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Hypotheses no edge \(C_I\)–\(C_G\) · Uses 6.10, 6.12

Statement (theory note, §6.6).

Proposition 6.13 (same least cost). Let \(\kappa^\star\) be the least \(\omega\) of a path from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) in \(U\). Every plan of \(U\) costs at least \(s\kappa^\star\), and the cheapest point \((s\kappa^\star, M^\star)\) of \(\mathcal F_{f(U)}\) costs exactly that. Hence \(U\) and \(f(U)\) have the same least cost, and \(\mathcal F_U = \mathrm{Min}\{(C, M) \in \mathcal A_U : M \le M^\star\}\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Take a feasible plan. Let \(Y(a, b)\) be its number of legs from \(a\) to \(b\), and \(Z(a, b)\) the number of these legs that belong to a journey. By capacity, \(0 \le Z \le Y\). Routes and journeys are walks from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\), so in the directed graph of \(U\), \(Y\) is an integer flow of value \(p\) and \(Z\) one of value \(t\) from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\), and \(Y - Z \ge 0\) is a flow of value \(t - p \ge 0\) from \(C_G\) to \(C_I\). By flow decomposition, \(Z\) is the sum of the arc sets of \(t\) paths from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) and of some cycles, and \(Y - Z\) the sum of those of \(t - p\) paths from \(C_G\) to \(C_I\) and of some cycles. Weight the arc from \(a\) to \(b\) by \(c(b) \ge 0\). The plan costs \(\sum Y(a, b)\,c(b)\). A path from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) weighs its \(\omega\), at least \(\kappa^\star\). A path from \(C_G\) to \(C_I\) weighs the landing costs of its vertices other than \(C_G\), which is \(\omega\) of the reversed path, as both hubs are free. So the plan costs at least \((2t - p)\kappa^\star\).

In \(f(U)\), every city costs \(\omega\) of the walk \(w\) followed by \(e\), at least \(\kappa^\star\): deleting closed sub-walks gives a path and removes landings. A cheapest path from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) has a central vertex, as there is no edge \(C_I\)–\(C_G\), and splitting it there gives a city of cost \(\kappa^\star\). A role configuration with passenger count \(t\) has \(s\) landings (Definition 4.2), so it costs at least \(s\kappa^\star\); \(s\) crossings at that city, split among the planes into odd numbers (\(s \ge p\), \(s \equiv p\)), cost exactly \(s\kappa^\star\). By Corollary 6.10 the cheapest point of \(\mathcal F_{f(U)}\) costs \(s\kappa^\star\), and by Proposition 6.12 it lies in \(\mathcal A_U\). It strictly dominates every point of \(\mathcal A_U\) with makespan above \(M^\star\), and such a point dominates no point with makespan at most \(M^\star\). This gives the last claim. \(\square\)

Proposition 6.16 (time-expanded flows). Let the durations be integers at least 1, and \(M\) an integer. In the network with nodes \((x, \tau)\) for the vertices \(x\) of \(U\) and \(0 \le \tau \le M\), waiting arcs from \((x, \tau)\) to \((x, \tau + 1)\), and flight arcs from \((a, \tau)\) to \((b, \tau + \mathrm{dur}(a, b))\) for every edge in both directions, take integer flows \(y\) of \(p\) units and \(z\) of \(t\) units from \((C_I, 0)\) to \((C_G, M)\), with \(z \le y\) on every flight arc and \(z = 0\) on the flight arcs into \(C_I\) and out of \(C_G\). Proof (theory note)

Proof. A plan of makespan at most \(M\) can be cleaned (Lemma 3.1) without changing its routes, and moved to integer times: durations are positive, so the plan is event-ordered (§1.2), and giving each leg, in that order, the earliest departure its route and its journey allow yields integer times, each no later than before. Each route, with waiting arcs in \(C_I\) before its first leg, between its legs, and after its last one up to \((C_G, M)\), is a unit path. So is each journey, with waiting arcs in \(C_I\) before its first leg, between its legs, and in \(C_G\) after its last. Journeys use distinct legs, so \(z \le y\); a clean journey has no flight into \(C_I\) and none out of \(C_G\); and the cost is the plan’s. Conversely, every arc moves forward in time, so \(y\) decomposes into \(p\) unit paths, the routes, and \(z\) into \(t\) unit paths. On each flight arc, give the passenger units of \(z\) distinct plane units among those of \(y\); this makes the journeys. Consecutive legs of a journey are joined in space and time, and if they belong to one plane the later one departs after the earlier one arrives, so it comes later on the route, as durations are at least 1. Each leg carries at most one passenger, every route ends in \(C_G\) by \(M\), and the plan costs \(\sum y\,c(\text{head})\).

Front. Every point of \(\mathcal A_U\) is weakly dominated by the point of a plan with integer times, so the points of \(\mathcal F_U\) have integer makespans, at most \(M^\star\) by Proposition 6.13. The argument of Proposition 7.1 then applies to \(R\): a point \((c, M)\) of the front has \(c = R(M) < R(M - 1)\), and conversely such a pair is attained with makespan exactly \(M\) and is dominated by no other point. \(\square\)

Let \(p = 2\), \(t = r + 1\) (so \(s = 2r\)) and \(T = B + rK\). Distinct cities make both groups odd (Lemma 2.4). If a subset of sum \(B\) exists, pad both sides with zero items to \(r\) items each, which \(r \ge m\) allows: two odd groups of duration \(B + rK\). Conversely, the two groups have duration exactly \(B + rK\) each, since they sum to \(2B + 2rK\). A group with \(r + 1\) items or more has duration at least \((r+1)K > B + rK\), so both groups have \(r\) items, and the original items of one of them sum to \(B\).

Algorithm for fixed \(p\): process the cities one at a time, with states made of the group durations (each in \([0, T]\)), the parities of \(|X_k|\) and of \(h_k\), and whether each group is non-empty: \((T+1)^p 8^p\) states. For city \(i\), choose how its \(X(i)\) copies split among the planes (\(O(s^{p-1})\) ways), and an even set of planes that received a copy, on which \(h_{k,i} = 1\) (at most \(2^{p-1}\) ways). Conditions (h1) and (h3) are local to the city, and (h2) is checked at the end. The time is \(O(n\,(T+1)^p\, 8^p\, s^{p-1}\, 2^p)\). \(\square\)

Lemma 9.2 (potentials)

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Uses 7.6

Statement (theory note, §9).

Lemma 9.2 (potentials) [X2]. In a finite graph with arc weights in \(\mathbb K\), let \(V\) map nodes to \(\mathbb K \cup \{\infty\}\) with \(V(\text{target}) \le 0\) and \(V(u) \le \mathrm{wt}(u,v) + V(v)\) on every arc. Then \(V(\text{source})\) is at most the weight of every path from the source to the target.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Along a path \(u_0, \dots, u_m\) ending at the target, \(V(u_m) \le 0\), and by backward induction \(V(u_j) \le \sum_{j' \ge j} \mathrm{wt}(u_{j'}, u_{j'+1})\). \(\square\)

Lemma 9.3 (default potentials)

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Uses 7.6

Statement (theory note, §9).

Lemma 9.3 (default potentials). At level \(T\), let \(G^a[m] = c_a + G[m-1][T - d_a]\), the cheapest \(m\)-group containing \(a\). Let \(L_{\rm rest}(p, s) = 0\), \(L_{\rm rest}(p, j) = \infty\) for \(j \ne s\), and \(L_{\rm rest}(k, j) = \min_m \big(G[m][T] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m)\big)\). Let \(L^a(p, j) = \infty\) and \(L^a(k, j) = \min_m \min\big(G^a[m] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m),\ G[m][T] + L^a(k+1, j+m)\big)\). Define \(V_0(k, j, \emptyset) = L_{\rm rest}(k, j)\) and \(V_0(k, j, \sigma) = \max_{a\in\sigma} L^a(k, j)\) for \(\sigma \ne \emptyset\). Then \(V_0(\text{target}) = 0\) and \(V_0(u) \le \mathrm{wt}(u, v) + V_0(v)\) on every arc of the σ-graph.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. By induction from \(k = p\), \(L^a \ge L_{\rm rest}\), since \(G^a[m] \ge G[m][T]\); so \(V_0(v) \ge L_{\rm rest}(v)\) at every node \(v\). An arc leaving \((k, j, \sigma)\) with option \((m, \eta)\) has weight at least \(G[m][T]\), and at least \(G^a[m]\) when \(a \in \eta\), because \(\eta\) with its filler is an \(m\)-group of duration at most \(T\). If \(\sigma = \emptyset\): \(V_0(u) = L_{\rm rest}(k, j) \le G[m][T] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\). If \(\sigma \ne \emptyset\) and \(a \in \sigma\): when \(a \in \eta\), \(L^a(k, j) \le G^a[m] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\); when \(a \notin \eta\), \(a \in \sigma \triangle \eta\), so \(V_0(v) \ge L^a(k+1, j+m)\) and \(L^a(k, j) \le G[m][T] + L^a(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\). Take the maximum over \(a\). \(\square\)

Remarks in the note

Sparse potential. The certificate lists some nodes \(u\) with values \(V(u)\). The checker sets \(V'(u) = \max(V(u), V_0(u))\) on listed nodes and \(V' = V_0\) elsewhere. It checks \(V'(\text{target}) \le 0\), \(V'(u) \le \mathrm{wt}(u, v) + V'(v)\) on every arc leaving a listed node, and \(V'(\text{source}) \ge C_i\). Arcs leaving other nodes satisfy the inequality by Lemma 9.3, as \(V' \ge V_0\). By Lemma 9.2 and Theorem 7.6, \(E(T^\star_i) \ge C_i\). The listed nodes are those that survive the solver’s pruning.

The linear family (default generator)

The generator’s default instances have costs affine in durations. §10 gives their front in closed form, valid whatever the central flights.

Theorem 10.1

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Hypotheses (S) · Uses 1.1, 2.9, 3.4, 4.1, 7.2, 7.10

Statement (theory note, §10).

Theorem 10.1. Assume (S), \(d_i = i\) and \(c_i = \alpha - \beta i\) for \(i = 1,\dots,n\), with \(\beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha \ge \beta n\). Let \(q_0 = \lfloor s/p\rfloor\) and \(\rho = s - pq_0\). For every integer \(T \ge \kappa\), \[L(T) = E(T) = \alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T), \qquad \Phi(T) = (p - \rho)\min(T,\ nq_0) + \rho\min(T,\ n(q_0+1)),\] and \(L(T) = E(T) = \infty\) for \(T < \kappa\). Consequently, whatever the central flights, the front is \[\mathcal F = \{(\alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T),\ 2T) : \kappa \le T \le n\kappa\} \text{ if } \beta > 0, \qquad \mathcal F = \{(\alpha s,\ 2\kappa)\} \text{ if } \beta = 0,\] with makespans in units of \(r_0\). For \(\beta > 0\) it has \((n-1)\kappa + 1\) points.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (1) Affine cost. \(c(Y) = \alpha |Y| - \beta\,\delta(Y)\). An \(m\)-multiset of cities can have any duration in \([m, nm]\): start from \(m\) copies of city 1 and raise one landing by one at a time. So by Lemma 7.2, \(G[m][T] = \alpha m - \beta\min(T, nm)\) for \(1 \le m \le T\), and \(G[m][T] = \infty\) for \(m > T\).

  1. Convexity. \(m \mapsto \min(T, nm)\) is concave, so \(m \mapsto G[m][T]\) is convex on \([1, T]\). A sum \(\sum_k G[m_k][T]\) with \(\sum_k m_k = s\) is then minimised by the balanced profile, with \(p - \rho\) groups of size \(q_0\) and \(\rho\) of size \(q_0 + 1\): if \(m_u \ge m_v + 2\), moving one landing from \(u\) to \(v\) does not increase the sum. The profile is feasible iff \(\kappa \le T\); otherwise \(L = E = \infty\) by Proposition 7.10(c). So \(L(T) = \sum_k G[m_k][T] = \alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T)\).

  2. Admissibility. Give every group of size \(m\) the same \(G\)-optimal multiset. Exactly one of \(q_0\) and \(q_0 + 1\) is even, so all even groups are equal, and Lemma 2.9 makes the partition admissible. It costs \(L(T)\), so \(E(T) \le L(T) \le E(T)\).

  3. Front. By Lemma 3.4 a plan with makespan \(M\) costs at least \(L(\lfloor M/2\rfloor) = E(\lfloor M/2\rfloor)\), and Theorem 4.1 achieves \((E(T), 2T)\); both need only (S). \(E\) is constant from \(n\kappa\) on, so the finite set \(\{(E(T), 2T) : \kappa \le T \le n\kappa\} \subseteq \mathcal A\) dominates \(\mathcal A\), and Lemma 1.1 gives \(\mathcal F\). For \(\kappa < T \le n\kappa\), \(\Phi(T) - \Phi(T-1)\) is \(p\) when \(T \le nq_0\) and \(\rho\) when \(nq_0 < T \le n(q_0+1)\); the second range meets \([\kappa, n\kappa]\) only if \(\rho \ge 1\). So for \(\beta > 0\) every such \(T\) is a breakpoint. \(\square\)

Remarks in the note

The count \((n-1)\kappa + 1\) matches the front sizes measured with the prototype on generator instances: 1,090 points for \(n = 100\), \(t = 60\), \(p = 10\); 540 for \(n = 50\) with the same \(t\) and \(p\); 399 for \(n = 200\), \(t = 3\), \(p = 2\). Neither link structures nor smoothing the free landings of a group are needed for this family.

What the theorem does not cover:

  • Offset progressions. For \(d_i = a + bi\) with \(a > 0\), \(G[\cdot][T]\) need not be convex and \(L < E\) occurs. With \(d = (5, 7, 9, 11)\), \(c_i = 24 - 2d_i\), \(t = 7\) and \(p = 4\), \(L(20) = 80 < E(20) = 84\) (by enumeration; 4 of 5,904 levels on 150 random offset instances). Contiguity from the step is essential: the two-city instance \(d = (6, 10)\) of §5 is affine as well.
  • The other generator families (logarithm, square root, \(2^i\), \((5/2)i\)) have no closed form; the general algorithm applies.
