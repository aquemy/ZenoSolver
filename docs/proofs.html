Blueprint

Every statement of the theory note, the papers’ versions, and the Lean declarations

This blueprint follows the theory note doc/theory/exact-front.md, which closes the gaps in the optimality arguments of Quemy & Schoenauer (EvoCOP 2015) and of Quemy, Schoenauer & Dréo (arXiv:2304.14659, 2023). For each node it gives, where they exist, the papers’ statement, the note’s statement and proof, and the Lean declarations of lean/ZenoFront, with their source.

Authorship. The grey boxes reproduce the papers verbatim, with their attribution, for scholarly comparison. The note, the Lean development and this page are the work of Alexandre Quemy. The statements and proofs in the blue boxes are cut out of the note when the site is built, and the listings out of the Lean sources, so neither can drift from the repository.

From the original paper (Quemy & Schoenauer, EvoCOP 2015, §2.1 Instances, HAL preprint hal-01109777v1, pp. 2–3).

Let us introduce some notations related to the planning problem briefly presented in the introduction: a MultiZenoTravel instance (Figure 1) is defined by the following elements:

– \(n\) central cities, organized as a clique in which every node is connected to \(C_I\) and \(C_G\), respectively the initial city and the goal city.
– \(c \in (\mathbb R^+)^n\), where \(c_i\) is the cost for landing in \(C_i\).
– \(D \in (\mathbb R^+)^{n\times n}\), where \(D_{ij}\) is the flying time between \(C_i\) and \(C_j\).
– \(d^I \in (\mathbb R^+)^n\), where \(d^I_i\) is the flying time between \(C_I\) and \(C_i\).
– \(d^G \in (\mathbb R^+)^n\), where \(d^G_i\) is the flying time between \(C_i\) and \(C_G\).
– \(p\) planes, initially in \(C_I\), that have a capacity of an unique person.
– \(t\) persons, initially in \(C_I\).

As said, the goal is to carry all \(t\) persons, initially in \(c_I\), to \(c_G\) using \(p\) planes, minimizing both the makespan and the cost of the plan. In order to ease the identification of the true Pareto Front, a symmetry constraint is added: \(\forall i \in [1, n], d^I_i = d^G_i\) and from thereon we will refer to a unique vector \(d\).

Statement (theory note, §1.1).

  • Cities: \(C_I\) (initial) and \(C_G\) (goal), the hubs, and central cities \(1,\dots,n\).
  • Flights are undirected: an existing flight joins two distinct cities and has one duration, used in both directions. \(C_I\)–\(i\) lasts \(d_i \ge 0\), \(i\)–\(C_G\) lasts \(\bar d_i \ge 0\), and a central flight \(i\)–\(j\) (\(i \ne j\)), if it exists, lasts \(d_{ij} = d_{ji} \ge 0\). There is no flight \(C_I\)–\(C_G\). \(\mathrm{dur}(a,b)\) denotes the duration of the flight \(a\)–\(b\).
  • Landing in central city \(i\) costs \(c_i \ge 0\); landing in a hub is free.
  • \(p \ge 1\) planes and \(t \ge p\) passengers start in \(C_I\) at time 0. A plane carries at most one passenger.
  • Durations and times lie in an archimedean ordered field \(\mathbb K\) (\(\mathbb Q\) or \(\mathbb R\)), so that \(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\) is defined. From §7 on, durations are integers.

In Lean. Network holds the durations d, dbar and an arbitrary table dc of central flights; costs are a separate argument c : Fin n → ℕ. Durations, costs and times are natural numbers (format v1); Cert.check_sound_rat extends the checker’s soundness to rational times.

Plans, cost and makespan

Lean Lean definition · Note definition

Statement (theory note, §1.2).

Definition (plan). A plan on a finite set \(Q\) of planes (all \(p\) planes unless stated otherwise) consists of

  1. for every plane \(k \in Q\), a route: a finite sequence of legs \((k,1),\dots,(k,\nu_k)\), where leg \((k,j)\) uses an existing flight joining \(v_{k,j-1}\) and \(v_{k,j}\), flown from \(v_{k,j-1}\) to \(v_{k,j}\), and departs at time \(e_{k,j}\), with \(v_{k,0} = C_I\), \(e_{k,1} \ge 0\) and \(e_{k,j+1} \ge a_{k,j} := e_{k,j} + \mathrm{dur}(v_{k,j-1}, v_{k,j})\);
  2. for every passenger \(\pi\), a journey: a non-empty sequence of legs whose first leg departs from \(C_I\), whose last leg arrives in \(C_G\), and in which each leg departs from the city where the previous one arrives, no earlier than that arrival, and, if both are legs of the same plane, at a later position of its route;
  3. capacity: the journeys are pairwise disjoint, and none repeats a leg.

Below, “flight” also means a leg when no confusion is possible. The plan is feasible if every route ends in \(C_G\) (\(\nu_k \ge 1\) and \(v_{k,\nu_k} = C_G\)). Its cost \(C = \sum_{k,j} c(v_{k,j})\) sums the landing costs of all flights (hubs are free). Its makespan \(M = \max_k a_{k,\nu_k}\) is the time at which the last plane reaches \(C_G\) for the last time. \(\mathcal A\) is the set of pairs \((C, M)\) of feasible plans.

Waiting is implicit: a departure may be later than the preceding arrival, for planes and for passengers. Boarding and debarking are instantaneous: a passenger changing planes debarks when its flight arrives and boards when the next one departs, and the only constraint is the time inequality of item 2. Several planes may be in a city at once.

Sequence order. “First” and “last” refer to positions in a journey or in a route. With zero durations several events can share a time; the sequences fix their order, and every argument below reads them this way.

A plan is event-ordered if its legs have a total order \(\prec\) that extends every route and every journey and along which departure times do not decrease. Lemma 6.8 and Corollary 6.10 use this notion, and Statement 1.2 relates it to PDDL; Theorem 6.9 does not need it. If every flight has a positive duration, every plan is event-ordered: in a route or a journey the next leg departs after the previous one arrives, hence strictly after it departs, so sorting legs by departure time extends both. Plans of the PDDL domain are event-ordered too (Statement 1.2). With zero durations a plan can fail to be: a passenger may ride a later leg of one plane and then an earlier leg of another at the same instant, in a cycle of dependencies that no execution in time realises. Such plans do not change the front (Corollary 6.10).

The flight time of plane \(k\) is \(F_k = \sum_j \mathrm{dur}(f_{k,j})\). Its flights are sequential, so \(F_k \le a_{k,\nu_k} \le M\).

Planes ending anywhere. In the variant of Theorem 4.9, every plan counts (routes need not end in \(C_G\) and may be empty), journeys still end in \(C_G\), and the makespan \(M'\) is the largest arrival time over all legs; there is at least one leg since \(t \ge 1\). \(\mathcal A'\) is the set of pairs \((C, M')\) of all plans.

In Lean. A plan gives every plane a list of legs (Leg: from, to, departure, arrival, passenger). Feasible requires routes chained from C_I at time ≥ 0 and ending in C_G, legs of exact duration, and every passenger’s legs ordered into a route from C_I to C_G; capacity one holds by construction, since a leg names at most one passenger.

Statement 1.2 (PDDL correspondence)

Lean informal · Note sketch

Statement (theory note, §1.3).

Statement 1.2 (PDDL correspondence, S). A plan of the domain under ε-separation yields a plan of §1.2 with the same cost and a makespan at most its total-time: each plane’s fly actions in time order form its route, each person’s flights between board and debark form its journey, and libre gives capacity one; ordering the fly actions by start time (ties by the plan’s order of actions) shows that this plan is event-ordered. Conversely, an event-ordered plan of §1.2 with makespan \(M\) yields PDDL plans with total-time at most \(M + K\varepsilon\), where \(K\) is its number of fly, board and debark actions: list the actions in the event order (each board just before its leg, each debark just after) and delay the \(r\)-th one by \(r\varepsilon\). So the PDDL front tends to the front of event-ordered plans, which is \(\mathcal F\) under (S) and (A1) (the plans of Theorem 4.1 are event-ordered, Lemma 6.8(c)) and under (A1-NS) (Corollary 6.10). The model’s makespan is thus the PDDL makespan in the limit ε → 0, which is what ZenoSolver reports. Whether PDDL planners and VAL accept zero-duration actions is not checked here. This is argued, not formalised.

Assumptions

Lean Lean definition · Note definition

From the original paper (Quemy & Schoenauer, EvoCOP 2015, §2.2 Pareto Optimal Plans, HAL preprint hal-01109777v1, pp. 3–4).

Let us make another simplifying assumption:
Assumption A1: \(\forall (i, j) \in [1, n]^2, d_i + d_j < d_{ij}\). Then the following holds.

Proposition: Pareto-optimal plans are plans where exactly \(2t - p\) (possibly identical) central cities are used by a flight.

Proof: Consider a plan where a person flies from \(C_i\) to \(C_j\). Using the same plane, the same person could fly instead from \(C_i\) to \(C_G\), and the plane would return empty to \(C_j\). The plan could continue unchanged from thereon: because of the hypothesis on makespans, the needed resource would be in \(C_j\) on time. Moreover, the total cost is unchanged, and the total makespan is lower or equal to the original one: the new plan thus Pareto-dominates the original one.

Iterating the same reasoning for each person, and each empty plane, we conclude that there are no flights between central cities in Pareto-optimal plans. Thus bringing the \(t\) persons from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) will amount to carry each person through one central city: \(t\) flights will be needed from \(C_I\) to one \(C_i\), then \(t\) flights from \(C_i\) to \(C_G\). Finally, because planes do not need to come back from \(C_G\) in the end, only \(t - p\) flights back empty will be needed, possibly through some different central cities – hence the result. \(\square\)

Footnote 3 (on Assumption A1): This might look unrealistic in real-world logistic domain. However, we hypothesize that the proposition still holds with the weaker condition that for any cities \(C_i, C_j, C_k\) (if we state the cost of \(C_I\) and \(C_G\) are respectively \(C_0\) and \(C_{n+1}\)), \(d_{ik} \le d_{ij} + d_{jk}\) (triangle inequality).

    1. symmetry: \(d_i = \bar d_i\) for all \(i\).

  • (A1-NS) \(d_{ij} \ge \min(d_i + d_j,\ \bar d_i + \bar d_j)\) for every existing central flight.
  • (AΔ) triangle inequality for the flights that exist: \(\mathrm{dur}(a,b) \le \mathrm{dur}(a,x) + \mathrm{dur}(x,b)\) whenever the three flights exist.

Lean Lean definition · Note definition

Statement (theory note, §1.5).

For points \(y, z \in \mathbb K^2\) (cost, makespan), \(y \preceq z\), \(y\) weakly dominates \(z\), if \(y \le z\) componentwise. For \(\mathcal S \subseteq \mathbb K^2\), \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\) is the set of \(z \in \mathcal S\) such that no \(y \in \mathcal S\) other than \(z\) has \(y \preceq z\). In Lean. IsParetoFront S F: the listed points are in S, every point of S is weakly dominated by a listed point, and the list is an antichain. IsParetoFront.mem_iff_minimal shows that it lists exactly the minimal elements of S.

Lemma 1.1 (dominating subset)

Lean informal (parts in Lean) · Note proof in the note

Statement (theory note, §1.5).

Lemma 1.1 (dominating subset). Let \(\mathcal S' \subseteq \mathcal S \subseteq \mathbb K^2\) with \(\mathcal S'\) finite and every point of \(\mathcal S\) weakly dominated by a point of \(\mathcal S'\). Then \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S = \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S'\), and every point of \(\mathcal S\) is weakly dominated by a point of \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Let \(z \in \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\). Some \(y \in \mathcal S'\) has \(y \preceq z\), so \(y = z\), and \(z\) is minimal in \(\mathcal S' \subseteq \mathcal S\). Let \(z \in \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S'\) and \(y \in \mathcal S\) with \(y \preceq z\). Some \(y' \in \mathcal S'\) has \(y' \preceq y \preceq z\), so \(y' = z\) and then \(y = z\). Finally, for \(z \in \mathcal S\), take \(y \in \mathcal S'\) with \(y \preceq z\) and a \(\preceq\)-minimal element of the finite set \(\{y' \in \mathcal S' : y' \preceq y\}\); it lies in \(\mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S' = \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal S\). \(\square\)

In Lean. The first claim, Min S = Min S', is machine-checked without the finiteness hypothesis. The last claim (every point is dominated by a minimal one) uses finiteness and is not stated in Lean.

Statement (theory note, §2).

Definition 2.2 (admissible partition). Let \(X_1,\dots,X_p\) be a partition of \(X\). A parity matrix for it is \(h \in \{0,1\}^{p\times n}\) such that

  • (h1) \(h_{k,i} = 1\) implies \(X_k(i) \ge 1\);
  • (h2) \(h_k := \sum_i h_{k,i} \equiv |X_k| + 1 \pmod 2\) for every plane \(k\);
  • (h3) \(\sum_k h_{k,i} \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) for every city \(i\).

The partition is admissible if it has a parity matrix. Write \(\eta_k = \{i : h_{k,i} = 1\}\), so that \(|\eta_k| = h_k\), and \(Z(h) = \bigcup_k \eta_k\) for the cities that \(h\) touches. A plane with \(|X_k|\) even is an even plane. An empty group would need \(h_k = 0 \equiv 1\) by (h1)–(h2), so admissible partitions have non-empty groups. Admissibility is decidable by enumerating \(h\) on the support of the groups. This definition replaces the component form of the first version (Lemma 2.4 shows they agree), and is the one to formalise.

Lemma 2.6 (link forest)

Lean machine-checked · Note proof in the note · Uses 2.3, 2.4

Statement (theory note, §2).

Lemma 2.6 (link forest) [LF]. A partition is admissible if and only if it has a link forest. An admissible partition has a link forest with at most one edge of each label at each plane.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (If.) Let \(\tilde h_{u,i}\) be the number of edges at \(u\) labelled \(i\). Then \(\tilde h_{u,i} \le X_u(i)\) by (F1), \(\sum_i \tilde h_{u,i} = \deg_\Lambda(u) \equiv |X_u| + 1\) by (F2), and \(\sum_u \tilde h_{u,i}\) is twice the number of edges labelled \(i\). Lemma 2.3 applies. This direction holds for any loopless multigraph (a loop would add 2 to the degree but 1 to \(\tilde h_{u,i}\)).

(Only if.) Take a spanning forest \(\Theta\) of \(H\) and a parity matrix with support \(J \subseteq E(\Theta)\) (Lemma 2.4(b)). At each city \(i\), \(\deg_J(i)\) is even. Split the planes \(J\)-adjacent to \(i\) into \(\deg_J(i)/2\) pairs, and for each pair \(\{u, v\}\) add to \(\Lambda\) an edge \(uv\) labelled \(i\). Then \(u \ne v\), since \(J\) has at most one edge per plane and city. At most one edge at \(u\) is labelled \(i\), and only if \((u,i) \in J\), hence \(X_u(i) \ge 1\): (F1) holds. \(\deg_\Lambda(u) = \deg_J(u) = h_u \equiv |X_u| + 1\): (F2) holds.

\(\Lambda\), built so far as a multigraph, has no cycle, and in particular no parallel edges. Take a cycle: a closed trail \(u_0, u_1, \dots, u_{m-1}, u_0\) with \(m \ge 2\) distinct edges \(u_{j-1}u_j\) labelled \(i_j\) (indices mod \(m\)), with distinct vertices if \(m \ge 3\); \(m = 2\) is the case of parallel edges. It gives the closed walk \(u_0, i_1, u_1, i_2, \dots, i_m, u_0\) in \(J\), which never uses the same edge twice in a row, even cyclically. At a city \(i_j\) it arrives from \(u_{j-1}\) and leaves to \(u_j \ne u_{j-1}\). At a plane \(u_j\) it arrives from \(i_j\) and leaves to \(i_{j+1}\); if \(i_j = i_{j+1}\), then \(u_j\) would belong to two different pairs at that city (the two distinct edges of the cycle at \(u_j\)). But a closed walk of positive length in a forest uses some edge twice in a row: at a vertex of the walk farthest from the root of its tree, both neighbours on the walk are its parent. \(\square\)

Theorem 4.1. Assume (S). Proof. Take a link forest \(\Lambda\) (Lemma 2.6) and root each of its trees at a plane. For a link at \(i\) between a parent \(u\) and a child \(v\), \(u\) will drop a passenger in \(i\) (a segment \(B\)) and \(v\) will pick it up (a segment \(\bar B\)). A non-root plane has exactly one parent link, so every plane picks up at most once.   2. Its \(r_k\) free landings as crossings \(A, \bar A, A, \dots, A\); each \(A\) carries a new passenger from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\). These never wait.

  1. A plan is event-ordered iff its next-leg relation has no cycle.

  2. In a simple plan with clean journeys, a cycle of the next-leg relation passes through a central city \(x\) with \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\).

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (a) An event order extends the relation, so the relation has no cycle. Conversely, along the relation departure times do not decrease, since the next leg departs no earlier than the previous one arrives; sorting the legs by departure time, ties broken by a topological order of the acyclic relation, gives an event order.

  1. Along each step the next leg departs no earlier than the previous one departs plus its duration, so every leg on a cycle has zero duration. A step of the relation either stays on one plane and goes forward on its route, or follows a link from the first leg of a drop-off (phase 1) to the second leg of a pick-up (phase 3). Phases never decrease along the relation, and they increase at every step between two planes. A cycle therefore has no such step and stays on one route, which is impossible. Theorem 6.9 (schedulable domination)

Statement (theory note, §6.2).

Theorem 6.9 (schedulable domination) [SD]. Assume (A1-NS). Let a feasible plan have cost \(C\) and makespan \(M\). Then there are a role configuration on all \(p\) planes with passenger count \(t\) and cost at most \(C\), orders of its drop-offs and pick-ups, and a link matching whose template has makespan at most \(M\). Neither an event order, nor (AΔ), nor (AΔ-path) is needed.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Step 1: a simple plan with clean journeys. Replace central flights and clean, as in the proof of Theorem 6.1; cost, arrival times, flight times and makespan do not increase. By Lemma 4.3(a), every journey is now a leg \(\alpha_\pi : C_I \to x\) followed by a leg \(\beta_\pi : x \to C_G\), which departs no earlier than \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives. Every route is a sequence of segments, one per landing. For a segment \(\gamma\) at a city \(x\), let \(a(\gamma)\) be the time its first leg arrives in \(x\) and \(e(\gamma)\) the time its second leg departs, so \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma)\). From here on only the routes and the pairs \((\alpha_\pi, \beta_\pi)\) matter: the moves below keep the routes valid, but need not keep a plan in the sense of item 2 of §1.2.

Step 2: rewiring. Next to the routes, keep a set of loops, empty at first: closed walks whose legs all depart and arrive at one time \(\tau\), so last \(0\), and alternate between hubs and central cities, so that a loop is made of segments too. A passenger is carried through if \(\alpha_\pi\) and \(\beta_\pi\) are the two legs of one segment, of a route or of a loop. Otherwise it is a hand-off, from the segment \(\gamma\) of \(\alpha_\pi\) to the segment \(\gamma'\) of \(\beta_\pi\), both at its city \(x\). While one of these moves applies, apply it.

  • Exchange. \(\gamma\) is on the route of \(u\), \(\gamma'\) on the route of \(v \ne u\), and \(a(\gamma') \le e(\gamma)\): \(v\) is in \(x\) before \(u\) leaves. Give \(u\) the legs of \(v\) from \(\beta_\pi\) on, and \(v\) the legs of \(u\) after \(\alpha_\pi\). Both routes stay valid: \(\beta_\pi\) departs after \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives, and the second leg of \(\gamma\) departs after the first leg of \(\gamma'\) arrives.
  • Cut. \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma'\) are on one route, \(\gamma'\) before \(\gamma\). The route gives \(e(\gamma') \le a(\gamma)\) and the journey \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma')\), so every leg from \(\beta_\pi\) to \(\alpha_\pi\) departs and arrives at \(\tau = e(\gamma')\) and lasts \(0\); in particular \(d_x = \bar d_x = 0\). These legs form a closed walk at \(x\). Remove it from the route, which keeps the first leg of \(\gamma'\) and the second leg of \(\gamma\), a segment at \(x\) since \(a(\gamma') \le \tau \le e(\gamma)\). The walk becomes a loop; its segments are \((\alpha_\pi, \beta_\pi)\) and the segments strictly between \(\gamma'\) and \(\gamma\).
  • Splice. One of \(\gamma, \gamma'\) is a segment \(F\) of a loop at time \(\tau\), the other a segment \(\delta\) of a route, and \(e(\gamma) > a(\gamma')\). Then \(a(\delta) < \tau \le e(\delta)\) or \(a(\delta) \le \tau < e(\delta)\): if \(F = \gamma\), then \(e(\gamma) = \tau > a(\delta)\) and \(\tau \le e(\delta)\) by the journey; if \(F = \gamma'\), then \(a(\delta) \le \tau\) by the journey and \(e(\delta) > \tau\). Insert the loop into the route at \(\delta\): the first leg of \(\delta\), the second leg of \(F\), the loop around to \(F\), the first leg of \(F\), the second leg of \(\delta\). The route stays valid, and \(\pi\) is carried through by the new segment of \(\alpha_\pi\) and \(\beta_\pi\).

Every move keeps the legs, so the cost, and every route’s last leg or exchanges two of them, so the makespan. It keeps the routes valid and the loops closed walks at one time, and it carries one more passenger through. It carries none less: the only segments it breaks are \(\gamma\) and \(\gamma'\), and a segment carrying another passenger through holds both of that passenger’s legs, so it is neither. So the moves stop, after at most \(t\) of them. Then every hand-off from \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma'\) has \(e(\gamma) \le a(\gamma')\):

  • between the routes of two planes, the exchange does not apply, so \(e(\gamma) < a(\gamma')\);
  • on one route, the cut does not apply, so \(\gamma'\) comes after \(\gamma\), and \(e(\gamma) \le a(\gamma')\) along the route;
  • between a loop and a route, the splice does not apply;
  • between two loops, at times \(\tau\) and \(\tau'\): \(e(\gamma) = \tau\) is when \(\alpha_\pi\) arrives and \(a(\gamma') = \tau'\) when \(\beta_\pi\) departs.

Step 3: paths. At each city \(x\), draw an arrow from \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma'\) for each hand-off at \(x\); the segments are those of the routes and of the loops. Each leg carries at most one passenger, so every segment has at most one outgoing and one incoming arrow. A \(B\) has no incoming arrow, since its second leg returns to \(C_I\), which clean journeys never reach, and a \(\bar B\) has no outgoing arrow. So the arrows form disjoint paths \(\gamma_0 \to \gamma_1 \to \dots \to \gamma_m\) with \(m \ge 1\), and cycles. The inner segments of a path, and all segments of a cycle, have an incoming and an outgoing arrow, so they are \(A\) segments, each dropping one passenger and picking up another. Along a path, by step 2, \(a(\gamma_0) \le e(\gamma_0) \le a(\gamma_1) \le \dots \le e(\gamma_m)\).

Step 4: selection. Attach each loop to any plane. Take every \(A\) and \(\bar A\) segment of each plane, on its route and its loops, as a crossing. A route from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) has \(\#A - \#\bar A = 1\) (Lemma 2.1) and a closed loop \(0\), so each plane gets an odd number of crossings. For each path from a \(B\) to a \(\bar B\), take its \(B\) as a drop-off and its \(\bar B\) as a pick-up, linked to each other. Take no other segment. The balance holds link by link. A passenger carried through rides an \(A\), which carries no arrow; every other passenger is an arrow of exactly one path or cycle. A cycle of \(k\) arrows consists of \(k\) \(A\) segments. A path with \(m\) arrows contributes its \(m - 1\) inner \(A\) segments, one more for each end that is an \(A\), and one link if it runs from a \(B\) to a \(\bar B\): at least \(m\) in every case. So the passenger count, the number of links plus the number of \(A\) segments, is at least \(t\). Distinct segments have distinct first legs, each a leg of the plan of step 1 landing at the segment’s city, so the selected segments are distinct landings of that plan and the cost is at most \(C\). A route segment is simple and lasts its role duration (Lemma 3.3(c)); a loop segment has role duration \(0\), since its legs last \(0\). So \(\lambda_k \le F_k \le M\), where \(F_k\) is the flight time of the rewired route of plane \(k\): it is valid, starts at time \(\ge 0\) and ends by \(M\).

Step 5: the closed form. The closed form \(M_{\rm tmpl}\) of Lemma 6.3 makes sense for any configuration, orders and matching. Order the drop-offs of each plane with those of its loops first, then those of its route in route order, and its pick-ups with those of its route in route order, then those of its loops. Use the links of step 4. Take a link from the \(j\)-th drop-off \(\gamma\) of \(u\) to the \(l\)-th pick-up \(\gamma'\) of \(v\), at \(x\). If \(\gamma\) is on the route, \(u\) flies its earlier route drop-offs before \(\gamma\), and the loop drop-offs weigh \(0\), so \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) + d_x \le a(\gamma)\); if \(\gamma\) is in a loop, \(\mathrm{Pre}_u(j) = d_x = 0\). If \(\gamma'\) is on the route, \(v\) flies \(\bar d_x\) to \(C_G\) after \(e(\gamma')\) and then its later route pick-ups, all by time \(M\), and the loop pick-ups weigh \(0\), so \(e(\gamma') + \bar d_x + \mathrm{Suf}_v(l) \le M\); if \(\gamma'\) is in a loop at time \(\tau\), \(\bar d_x = \mathrm{Suf}_v(l) = 0\) and \(e(\gamma') = \tau \le M\): every leg of a loop was a leg of a route before that route’s last leg, which arrives by \(M\). As \(a(\gamma) \le e(\gamma')\) (step 3), the term of the link is at most \(M\). Together with \(\lambda_k \le M\), \(M_{\rm tmpl} \le M\).

Step 6: shrink. Lemma 4.4 (\(t \ge p\), §1.1) brings the passenger count to \(t\), deleting two crossings of a plane or a drop-off together with a pick-up at its city. Take the pick-up linked to it, so that the remaining links still match drop-offs and pick-ups city by city. With the same orders, deleting crossings only lowers loads, and deleting a link lowers loads and the \(\mathrm{Pre}\) and \(\mathrm{Suf}\) of the other links, so \(M_{\rm tmpl}\) stays at most \(M\). The configuration now has passenger count \(t\), and by Lemma 6.3 its template has makespan \(M_{\rm tmpl} \le M\). \(\square\)

Complexity

§8 explains why no fast per-PPP rule can be exact, and why the dynamic programs are pseudo-polynomial. None of it is formalized.

Theorem 8.1

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Hypotheses \(p\) in the input · Uses 2.4

Statement (theory note, §8).

Theorem 8.1. Deciding \(M(X) \le 2T\), with \(d\), \(X\), \(p\) and \(T\) as input, is strongly NP-complete.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Membership: guess the partition and a parity matrix, and check Definition 2.2. Hardness, from 3-Partition, which is strongly NP-complete: given integers \(a_1,\dots,a_{3m}\) with \(B/4 < a_i < B/2\) and \(\sum_i a_i = mB\), can they be split into \(m\) triples of sum \(B\)? Take \(3m\) cities with \(d_i = a_i\), \(p = m\) and \(t = 2m\) (so \(s = 3m\)), \(X\) containing every city once, and \(T = B\). Each city lies in one group, so every component of \(H\) is one plane with its cities, and admissibility makes every group odd (Lemma 2.4). A 3-partition gives \(m\) triples, odd groups of duration \(B\). Conversely, groups of duration at most \(B\) summing to \(mB\) all have duration exactly \(B\); a group of one or two items has duration below \(B\) and one of four or more above \(B\), so all groups are triples. The numbers are those of the 3-Partition instance, so the reduction preserves strong NP-hardness. \(\square\)

Proposition 8.2 (fixed \(p\))

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Uses 2.2, 2.4

Statement (theory note, §8).

Proposition 8.2 (fixed \(p\)). For \(p = 2\) the problem stays NP-complete, even with positive durations. For every fixed \(p\) it is solvable in time polynomial in \(n\), \(s\) and \(T\). So for fixed \(p\) it is weakly NP-complete.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. Hardness, from Partition: given \(a_1,\dots,a_m\) with sum \(2B\), is there a subset of sum \(B\)? Choose an odd \(r \ge m\), add \(2r - m\) zero items, and give each of the \(2r\) items its own city of duration \(a_i + K\), with \(K = B + 1\). Let \(p = 2\), \(t = r + 1\) (so \(s = 2r\)) and \(T = B + rK\). Distinct cities make both groups odd (Lemma 2.4). If a subset of sum \(B\) exists, pad both sides with zero items to \(r\) items each, which \(r \ge m\) allows: two odd groups of duration \(B + rK\). Conversely, the two groups have duration exactly \(B + rK\) each, since they sum to \(2B + 2rK\). A group with \(r + 1\) items or more has duration at least \((r+1)K > B + rK\), so both groups have \(r\) items, and the original items of one of them sum to \(B\).

Algorithm for fixed \(p\): process the cities one at a time, with states made of the group durations (each in \([0, T]\)), the parities of \(|X_k|\) and of \(h_k\), and whether each group is non-empty: \((T+1)^p 8^p\) states. For city \(i\), choose how its \(X(i)\) copies split among the planes (\(O(s^{p-1})\) ways), and an even set of planes that received a copy, on which \(h_{k,i} = 1\) (at most \(2^{p-1}\) ways). Conditions (h1) and (h3) are local to the city, and (h2) is checked at the end. The time is \(O(n\,(T+1)^p\, 8^p\, s^{p-1}\, 2^p)\). \(\square\)

Lemma 9.2 (potentials) [X2]. In a finite graph with arc weights in \(\mathbb K\), let \(V\) map nodes to \(\mathbb K \cup \{\infty\}\) with \(V(\text{target}) \le 0\) and \(V(u) \le \mathrm{wt}(u,v) + V(v)\) on every arc. Lemma 9.3 (default potentials)

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Uses 7.6

Statement (theory note, §9).

Lemma 9.3 (default potentials). At level \(T\), let \(G^a[m] = c_a + G[m-1][T - d_a]\), the cheapest \(m\)-group containing \(a\). Let \(L_{\rm rest}(p, s) = 0\), \(L_{\rm rest}(p, j) = \infty\) for \(j \ne s\), and \(L_{\rm rest}(k, j) = \min_m \big(G[m][T] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m)\big)\). Let \(L^a(p, j) = \infty\) and \(L^a(k, j) = \min_m \min\big(G^a[m] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m),\ G[m][T] + L^a(k+1, j+m)\big)\). Define \(V_0(k, j, \emptyset) = L_{\rm rest}(k, j)\) and \(V_0(k, j, \sigma) = \max_{a\in\sigma} L^a(k, j)\) for \(\sigma \ne \emptyset\). Then \(V_0(\text{target}) = 0\) and \(V_0(u) \le \mathrm{wt}(u, v) + V_0(v)\) on every arc of the σ-graph.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. By induction from \(k = p\), \(L^a \ge L_{\rm rest}\), since \(G^a[m] \ge G[m][T]\); so \(V_0(v) \ge L_{\rm rest}(v)\) at every node \(v\). An arc leaving \((k, j, \sigma)\) with option \((m, \eta)\) has weight at least \(G[m][T]\), and at least \(G^a[m]\) when \(a \in \eta\), because \(\eta\) with its filler is an \(m\)-group of duration at most \(T\). If \(\sigma = \emptyset\): \(V_0(u) = L_{\rm rest}(k, j) \le G[m][T] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\). If \(\sigma \ne \emptyset\) and \(a \in \sigma\): when \(a \in \eta\), \(L^a(k, j) \le G^a[m] + L_{\rm rest}(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\); when \(a \notin \eta\), \(a \in \sigma \triangle \eta\), so \(V_0(v) \ge L^a(k+1, j+m)\) and \(L^a(k, j) \le G[m][T] + L^a(k+1, j+m) \le \mathrm{wt} + V_0(v)\). Theorem 10.1

Lean informal · Note proof in the note · Hypotheses (S) · Uses 1.1, 2.9, 3.4, 4.1, 7.2, 7.10

Statement (theory note, §10).

Theorem 10.1. Assume (S), \(d_i = i\) and \(c_i = \alpha - \beta i\) for \(i = 1,\dots,n\), with \(\beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha \ge \beta n\). Let \(q_0 = \lfloor s/p\rfloor\) and \(\rho = s - pq_0\). For every integer \(T \ge \kappa\), \[L(T) = E(T) = \alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T), \qquad \Phi(T) = (p - \rho)\min(T,\ nq_0) + \rho\min(T,\ n(q_0+1)),\] and \(L(T) = E(T) = \infty\) for \(T < \kappa\). Consequently, whatever the central flights, the front is \[\mathcal F = \{(\alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T),\ 2T) : \kappa \le T \le n\kappa\} \text{ if } \beta > 0, \qquad \mathcal F = \{(\alpha s,\ 2\kappa)\} \text{ if } \beta = 0,\] with makespans in units of \(r_0\). For \(\beta > 0\) it has \((n-1)\kappa + 1\) points.

Proof (theory note)

Proof. (1) Affine cost. \(c(Y) = \alpha |Y| - \beta\,\delta(Y)\). An \(m\)-multiset of cities can have any duration in \([m, nm]\): start from \(m\) copies of city 1 and raise one landing by one at a time. So by Lemma 7.2, \(G[m][T] = \alpha m - \beta\min(T, nm)\) for \(1 \le m \le T\), and \(G[m][T] = \infty\) for \(m > T\).

  1. Convexity. \(m \mapsto \min(T, nm)\) is concave, so \(m \mapsto G[m][T]\) is convex on \([1, T]\). A sum \(\sum_k G[m_k][T]\) with \(\sum_k m_k = s\) is then minimised by the balanced profile, with \(p - \rho\) groups of size \(q_0\) and \(\rho\) of size \(q_0 + 1\): if \(m_u \ge m_v + 2\), moving one landing from \(u\) to \(v\) does not increase the sum. The profile is feasible iff \(\kappa \le T\); otherwise \(L = E = \infty\) by Proposition 7.10(c). So \(L(T) = \sum_k G[m_k][T] = \alpha s - \beta\,\Phi(T)\).

  2. Admissibility. Give every group of size \(m\) the same \(G\)-optimal multiset. Exactly one of \(q_0\) and \(q_0 + 1\) is even, so all even groups are equal, and Lemma 2.9 makes the partition admissible. It costs \(L(T)\), so \(E(T) \le L(T) \le E(T)\).

  3. Front. By Lemma 3.4 a plan with makespan \(M\) costs at least \(L(\lfloor M/2\rfloor) = E(\lfloor M/2\rfloor)\), and Theorem 4.1 achieves \((E(T), 2T)\); both need only (S). \(E\) is constant from \(n\kappa\) on, so the finite set \(\{(E(T), 2T) : \kappa \le T \le n\kappa\} \subseteq \mathcal A\) dominates \(\mathcal A\), and Lemma 1.1 gives \(\mathcal F\). For \(\kappa < T \le n\kappa\), \(\Phi(T) - \Phi(T-1)\) is \(p\) when \(T \le nq_0\) and \(\rho\) when \(nq_0 < T \le n(q_0+1)\); the second range meets \([\kappa, n\kappa]\) only if \(\rho \ge 1\). So for \(\beta > 0\) every such \(T\) is a breakpoint. \(\square\)

Remarks in the note

The count \((n-1)\kappa + 1\) matches the front sizes measured with the prototype on generator instances: 1,090 points for \(n = 100\), \(t = 60\), \(p = 10\); 540 for \(n = 50\) with the same \(t\) and \(p\); 399 for \(n = 200\), \(t = 3\), \(p = 2\). Neither link structures nor smoothing the free landings of a group are needed for this family.

Contiguity from the step is essential: the two-city instance \(d = (6, 10)\) of §5 is affine as well.

  • The other generator families (logarithm, square root, \(2^i\), \((5/2)i\)) have no closed form; the general algorithm applies.
