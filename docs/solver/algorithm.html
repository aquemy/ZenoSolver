Algorithm

How zeno computes an exact Pareto front, step by step, on one small instance

This page explains how zeno computes the Pareto front of a MultiZenoTravel instance, and why the front it prints is exact. It can be read on its own: every symbol is defined here, and every claim cites the result that proves it. A citation links to the entry of the blueprint that states and proves the result; the badge after it says how much of it Lean checks (machine-checked, parts in Lean, Lean definition, or informal when the proof is on paper only). The proofs themselves are in the theory note. For context, see the problem page, which introduces the same example, and the paper section.

The page follows one instance from start to finish. Every number quoted for it, and every value in the figures, is recomputed from the definitions by site/gen_algorithm.py and checked against zeno (how).

The setting

Instances and plans. An instance has two hubs, \(C_I\), where every plane and passenger starts, and \(C_G\), where they must all end, and \(n\) central cities \(1, \dots, n\) §1.1 Lean definition. A flight between \(C_I\) and city \(i\) lasts \(d_i\), a flight between city \(i\) and \(C_G\) lasts \(\bar d_i\), and no flight joins the two hubs. Central cities may also be joined by flights of their own. Landing in city \(i\) costs \(c_i \ge 0\); landing in a hub is free. There are \(p\) planes and \(t \ge p\) passengers, and a plane carries at most one passenger at a time. A plan gives every plane a timed route and every passenger a journey. Its cost is the sum of its landing costs, and its makespan is the time at which the last plane reaches \(C_G\) §1.2 Lean definition.

The front. A point \((C, M)\) weakly dominates \((C', M')\) if \(C \le C'\) and \(M \le M'\). The Pareto front \(\mathcal F\) is the set of (cost, makespan) points of feasible plans that no other such point weakly dominates §1.5 Lean definition. zeno prints it as cost makespan lines.

Assumptions §1.4 Lean definition. Most of this page assumes

  • (S), symmetry: \(d_i = \bar d_i\) for every city, so a flight between city \(i\) and either hub lasts \(d_i\);
  • and either (A1): every central flight between \(i\) and \(j\) lasts at least \(d_i + d_j\), so it is never faster than going through a hub; or (AΔ): the flight durations satisfy the triangle inequality.

Instances with \(d \ne \bar d\) have their own pipeline, described at the end.

Notation

Symbol Meaning
\(s = 2t - p\) the least number of landings in central cities of a feasible plan (step 1)
\(\kappa = \lceil s/p \rceil\) the size of the largest group when \(s\) landings are spread over \(p\) planes as evenly as possible
\(X\) a multiset of central cities: the landings. \(X(i)\) is the multiplicity of city \(i\), \(\lvert X \rvert\) the size
\(c(X)\), \(\delta(X)\) \(\sum_{i \in X} c_i\) and \(\sum_{i \in X} d_i\), counted with multiplicity
\(\ell(X) = 2\delta(X)\) the load: the flight time of a plane that serves each landing of \(X\) by its own round trip from a hub
\(X_1, \dots, X_p\) a partition of \(X\): one group per plane, with \(X_1 + \dots + X_p = X\)
even plane a plane whose group has an even number of landings
\(g\) the gcd of the durations; durations are divided by it
\(T\) a level: an integer bound on every \(\delta(X_k)\), hence on every load, \(2gT\) in the original units
\(E(T)\) the least cost of an admissible partition within level \(T\) (step 4)
\(L(T)\), \(U(T)\), \(U_3(T)\) a lower bound and two upper bounds on \(E(T)\) (step 7)
\(G[m][\tau]\) the least cost of \(m\) landings whose durations sum to at most \(\tau\) (step 6)
\(\hat m = s - p + 1\) the largest group that can occur, every group being non-empty
\(q_{\max} = \min(p,\ t - p + 1)\) the most planes a useful link tree has (step 3)
\(w = \min(p - 1,\ t - p)\) the most cities a parity matrix needs to touch (step 9)
\(T_{\rm lo}\), \(T_{\rm hi}\) the first level with a plan, and the first level at the cheapest cost (step 4)

\(s\), \(\kappa\), \(c\), \(\delta\) and \(\ell\) are the notation of the theory note Notation informal.

The worked example

The instance of the problem page (site/problem/example.dat) has \(n = 3\) cities, \(t = 3\) passengers and \(p = 2\) planes:

city \(i\) 1 2 3
duration \(d_i = \bar d_i\) 2 3 5
landing cost \(c_i\) 9 6 1

City 1 is fast and expensive, city 3 slow and cheap. So \(s = 2 \cdot 3 - 2 = 4\) landings, \(\kappa = 2\) and \(g = 1\). The instance has no central flight table, and the default central flights of zeno satisfy (A1). Its front, as zeno -n 3 -t 3 -p 2 -d site/problem/example.dat prints it:

makespan 8 10 12 14 16 20
cost 36 30 24 20 14 4

The rest of the page derives this list. Part one says what is computed; part two says how.

What is computed

Step 1. From plans to landing multisets

Idea. A plan is a timed object, with routes, journeys and waiting, but its point on the front depends on two numbers only. The first step replaces plans by a finite, untimed object: the multiset of central cities the planes land in, split among the planes. Under (S), a landing in city \(i\) served by its own round trip from a hub (\(C_I \to i \to C_G\), \(C_G \to i \to C_I\), \(C_I \to i \to C_I\) or \(C_G \to i \to C_G\)) takes exactly \(2d_i\) Lemma 3.3 informal, parts in Lean. If every landing is flown this way, plane \(k\) flies for \(\ell(X_k) = 2\delta(X_k)\), and the question becomes which landings to make and how to split them.

Results.

  1. Counting. Every feasible plan lands at least \(s = 2t - p\) times in central cities Lemma 3.2 machine-checked. Every passenger needs a flight out of \(C_I\) and a flight into \(C_G\). Cut each route at its visits to the hubs: each piece, a segment, lands in a central city, and a plane leaves \(C_I\) one time more than it returns there. Counting the arrivals in the hubs gives at least \((t - p) + t\) segments.
  2. Achievability. For every multiset \(X\) with \(|X| = s\) and every admissible partition \(X_1, \dots, X_p\) of it (step 2), there is a feasible plan whose central landings are exactly \(X\), hence of cost \(c(X)\), that uses no central flight, in which plane \(k\) flies for \(\ell(X_k)\), and whose makespan is \(\max_k \ell(X_k)\) Theorem 4.1 machine-checked. Step 11 shows the construction.
  3. Converse. Under (S) and (A1), every feasible plan with cost \(C\) and makespan \(M\) is matched by an admissible partition of some \(X\) with \(|X| = s\), \(c(X) \le C\) and \(\ell(X_k) \le M\) for every plane Theorem 4.7 machine-checked. The same holds under (S) and (AΔ) Theorem 4.10 machine-checked.
  4. Together. Let \(M(X)\) be the least \(\max_k \ell(X_k)\) over the admissible partitions of \(X\), or \(\infty\) if there is none. Under (S) and (A1), \[\mathcal F = \mathrm{Min}\,\mathcal X, \qquad \mathcal X = \{(c(X), M(X)) : |X| = s,\ M(X) < \infty\},\] where \(\mathrm{Min}\) keeps the points that no other point of the set weakly dominates, and every front point is attained by a plan with exactly \(s\) central landings Corollary 4.8 machine-checked. The same holds under (S) and (AΔ) Corollary 4.12 informal, parts in Lean.

Some condition on the central flights is needed: with fast central flights that satisfy neither (A1) nor (AΔ), a plan can beat every point of \(\mathcal X\) Example 4.13 informal.

In the example. \(s = 4\). The multiset \(\{3, 3, 3, 3\}\) split as \(\{3, 3\} \mid \{3, 3\}\) costs \(4\), each plane has \(\delta = 10\), and the makespan is \(20\): the point \((4, 20)\). The multiset \(\{1, 1, 1, 1\}\) split as \(\{1, 1\} \mid \{1, 1\}\) costs \(36\) with makespan \(8\): the point \((36, 8)\). The four other points come from multisets in between.

In the code. solveFront (src/zeno/solve.cpp) takes symmetric instances and sends the others to solveBracket (below). checkFlights (include/zeno/flights.hpp) decides (A1), (AΔ) and (A1-NS) in exact integer arithmetic. With a central flight table that satisfies neither (A1) nor (AΔ), zeno still prints achieved points but warns that the front may not be exact.

Step 2. Admissible partitions

Idea. Not every split of \(X\) among the planes can be flown with loads \(\ell(X_k)\). In the plans of step 1, every landing is a round trip from a hub of one of four kinds: a crossing \(C_I \to i \to C_G\) carrying a passenger, or its empty return \(C_G \to i \to C_I\); a drop-off \(C_I \to i \to C_I\), which leaves a passenger in city \(i\); and a pick-up \(C_G \to i \to C_G\), which collects one there. A plane starts in \(C_I\) and ends in \(C_G\), so it flies an odd number of crossings, counting both directions Lemma 2.1 informal, parts in Lean. Every drop-off in city \(i\) is matched by a pick-up in city \(i\) Definition 4.2 Lean definition. So a plane with an even number of landings must take part in a hand-off, and hand-offs pair up city by city. The parity matrix records exactly this: \(h_{k,i}\) is the parity of the number of drop-offs and pick-ups of plane \(k\) in city \(i\) Lemma 4.5 machine-checked.

Definition Definition 2.2 Lean definition. A parity matrix for a partition \(X_1, \dots, X_p\) is a 0/1 matrix \(h\) with one row per plane and one column per city such that

  • (h1) \(h_{k,i} = 1\) only if plane \(k\) lands in city \(i\), that is \(X_k(i) \ge 1\);
  • (h2) row \(k\) has \(|X_k| + 1\) ones modulo 2: an odd number for an even plane, an even number for an odd plane;
  • (h3) every column has an even number of ones.

The partition is admissible if it has a parity matrix.

Equivalent forms.

  • Counting with multiplicities changes nothing: integer entries \(0 \le \tilde h_{k,i} \le X_k(i)\) with the same two parity conditions exist iff a parity matrix does Lemma 2.3 informal. This is the form a link forest produces (step 3).
  • Component form. Let \(H\) be the bipartite graph on planes and cities with an edge between plane \(k\) and city \(i\) whenever \(X_k(i) \ge 1\). The partition is admissible iff every connected component of \(H\) contains an even number of even planes Lemma 2.4 informal. This is the quickest test by hand.
  • Every row has at most \(|X_k| - 1\) ones Lemma 2.5 machine-checked: each plane keeps at least one landing outside the hand-offs, and an admissible partition has no empty group.
  • A sufficient condition: if the groups are non-empty, their sizes add up to \(p\) modulo 2, and some city belongs to every even group, the partition is admissible Lemma 2.9 informal.

In the example (Figure 1). The multiset \(\{2, 2, 3, 3\}\) split as \(\{2, 3\} \mid \{2, 3\}\): both planes are even, so each row needs an odd number of ones, and marking city 2 in both rows gives the column sums \(0, 2, 0\). The split is admissible. It costs \(14\), both planes have \(\delta = 8\), and the makespan is \(16\): the front point \((14, 16)\). The split \(\{1, 3\} \mid \{2, 2\}\) of \(\{1, 2, 2, 3\}\) is not: plane 2 is even and lands only in city 2, so its row must mark city 2; plane 1 is even and lands in cities 1 and 3, so its row marks exactly one of them; column 2 then holds a single one. The graph \(H\) shows it at a glance: plane 2 and city 2 form a component with one even plane. This split is not a candidate in Corollary 4.8, whatever its cost (22) and loads.

In the code. zeno never enumerates parity matrices. Its dynamic programs only build partitions that carry a link forest (step 3), which are admissible by construction. Admissibility is still decidable by enumerating \(h\) on the supports of the groups Definition 2.2 Lean definition; gen_algorithm.py does so over every partition of the example to check the figures of this page.

Step 3. Link forests

Idea. A parity matrix says which landings take part in hand-offs, not who hands off to whom. A link forest says it. Draw an edge between planes \(u\) and \(v\), labelled with city \(i\), for each passenger that one of them drops off in city \(i\) and the other picks up there. Each edge uses one landing in \(i\) of both planes. The other landings of a plane are its free landings, flown as crossings.

Definition Definition (link forest) Lean definition. A link forest for \(X_1, \dots, X_p\) is a forest \(\Lambda\) on the planes whose edges carry city labels, such that

  • (F1) for every plane \(u\) and city \(i\), at most \(X_u(i)\) edges at \(u\) are labelled \(i\);
  • (F2) \(r_u = |X_u| - \deg_\Lambda(u)\), the number of free landings of \(u\), is odd.

Results.

  • A partition is admissible iff it has a link forest, and then it has one with at most one edge of each label at each plane Lemma 2.6 machine-checked. In one direction, counting the edges of each label at each plane gives the integer form of Lemma 2.3. In the other, the forest is built from a parity matrix whose ones lie on a spanning forest of \(H\).
  • With \(|X| = s\), a link forest has at most \(t - p\) edges, so each of its trees has at most \(q_{\max} = \min(p, t - p + 1)\) planes Corollary 2.7 informal. Every plane keeps at least one free landing, so the degrees add up to at most \(s - p = 2(t - p)\).

Forests matter for two reasons. They can be built tree by tree, from the leaves up, by dynamic programming (step 8). And in the plan built from a forest, each plane picks up at most once, from its parent, which is what keeps waiting harmless (step 11).

In the example (Figure 2). \(t - p = 1\), so there is at most one link, and \(q_{\max} = 2\): every tree is a single plane or a pair. For the point \((14, 16)\), one edge labelled city 2 joins plane 1 to plane 2. Each plane keeps one free landing, in city 3: \(r_u = 2 - 1 = 1\), which is odd. In the plan, plane 1 drops passenger 1 in city 2 and plane 2 collects it.

In the code. LinkForest and ForestPlane (include/zeno/forest.hpp) store, for each plane, its filler (the odd number of free landings), its parent, and the label of the edge to it. The group of a plane is its filler, plus its parent’s label, plus one landing per child edge.

Step 4. The level function \(E(T)\) and its breakpoints

Idea. Corollary 4.8 still asks for the Pareto set of a search with two objectives. Turn it into a family of searches with one: fix a budget \(T\) for every plane, and ask for the cheapest admissible way to fit in it. As the budget grows, the least cost can only drop, and each drop is a front point.

Definition. Durations are integers, divided by their gcd \(g\) (rational durations are scaled to integers first). For an integer level \(T \ge 0\), \[E(T) = \min\{c(X) : |X| = s,\ X_1, \dots, X_p \text{ admissible},\ \delta(X_k) \le T \text{ for every } k\},\] with \(\min \emptyset = \infty\). A larger \(T\) allows more partitions, so \(E\) is non-increasing.

Results.

  • Under (S) and (A1), the front is the set of breakpoints of \(E\) Proposition 7.1 machine-checked: \[\mathcal F = \{(E(T),\ 2gT) : E(T) < E(T - 1)\}, \qquad E(-1) = \infty.\] Under (S) and (AΔ) as well Corollary 4.12 informal, parts in Lean.
  • Only a bounded range of levels matters Proposition 7.10 informal, parts in Lean. Let \(d_{\min}\) be the smallest duration, \(c_{\min}\) the smallest cost, and \(d_{\rm cheap}\) the smallest duration among the cities of cost \(c_{\min}\). Then \(E(T) < \infty\) iff \(T \ge T_{\rm lo} = d_{\min}\kappa\), and \(E(T) = s\,c_{\min}\) iff \(T \ge T_{\rm hi} = d_{\rm cheap}\kappa\). In the original units, every plan has makespan at least \(2d_{\min}\kappa\), which some plan attains, and the cheapest point of the front is \((s\,c_{\min},\ 2d_{\rm cheap}\kappa)\). Both hold under (S), whatever the central flights. The cost of the fastest point has no closed form: it is \(E(T_{\rm lo})\).

In the example (Figure 3). \(T_{\rm lo} = 2 \cdot 2 = 4\) and \(T_{\rm hi} = 5 \cdot 2 = 10\): seven levels. On \(T = 4, \dots, 10\), \(E\) takes the values 36, 30, 24, 20, 14, 14 and 4. It drops at every level but \(T = 9\), where the cheapest groups within the budget are still \(\{2, 3\} \mid \{2, 3\}\): no plan of makespan 18 is cheaper than the plan of makespan 16. Six breakpoints, six front points.

In the code. ReducedInstance::lowestLevel() and highestLevel() (include/zeno/reduce.hpp) give \(T_{\rm lo}\) and \(T_{\rm hi}\), and makespanPerLevel is \(2g\). Once every level is settled, extract() in solve.cpp emits a front point wherever \(E\) drops, and checks that \(E\) never rises.

Step 5. Reduce the instance

Idea. A city that is no faster and no cheaper than another is never needed: replacing each of its landings by a landing in the other city keeps the partition admissible (merge the two columns of the parity matrix) and raises neither the cost nor a load.

Result Proposition 7.9 informal, parts in Lean. If \(d_{i'} \le d_i\) and \(c_{i'} \le c_i\) for two cities \(i \ne i'\), removing city \(i\) leaves \(E(T)\) and \(L(T)\) unchanged at every level, and under (S) and (A1) it leaves the front unchanged. Applied repeatedly, keeping one city per class of equal \((d, c)\), the cities that remain form a staircase: durations strictly increasing, costs strictly decreasing.

In the example. \(2 < 3 < 5\) and \(9 > 6 > 1\): the cities already form a staircase, and \(g = 1\). Nothing changes.

In the code. reduce (src/zeno/reduce.cpp) merges equal cities, drops dominated ones, divides the durations by \(g\), and computes the level range. zeno --stats prints the result, here levels 4..10 (x1, 7 levels), cities 3, max tree 2.

Step 6. The one-plane table

Idea. Every bound and every exact method below keeps asking one question: what is the cheapest way for a single plane to make \(m\) landings within a budget \(\tau\)? It is a knapsack with exactly \(m\) items, in which each city can be used any number of times, and one table answers it for all \(m\) and \(\tau\) at once.

Result Lemma 7.2 machine-checked. Let \(G[0][\tau] = 0\) for \(\tau \ge 0\), \(G[m][\tau] = \infty\) for \(\tau < 0\), and \[G[m][\tau] = \min_{i:\ d_i \le \tau} \big(c_i + G[m-1][\tau - d_i]\big).\] Then \(G[m][\tau]\) is the least cost of a multiset of \(m\) cities whose durations sum to at most \(\tau\), and it is non-increasing in \(\tau\).

In the example (Figure 5). \(m\) runs up to \(\hat m = 3\) and \(\tau\) up to \(T_{\rm hi} = 10\). For instance \(G[2][8] = 7\): one landing in city 2 and one in city 3, of durations \(3 + 5 = 8\). Replay the fill to see each cell computed from a cell of the row above.

Step 7. The bracket

Idea. Before solving a level exactly, sandwich \(E(T)\) between a cheap lower bound and cheap upper bounds, \(L \le E \le U_3 \le U\). All three are knapsacks that combine small gadgets (a few planes with their groups) filled with \(G\)-optimal landings. Where the lower bound meets an upper bound, the level is settled.

The bounds. A knapsack over planes and landings combines gadgets, with \(\mathrm{For}[0][0] = 0\) and \[\mathrm{For}[P][J] = \min\big(\mathrm{For}[P - q][J - j] + \text{cost}\big)\] over the gadgets of \(q\) planes, \(j\) landings and that cost; the bound is \(\mathrm{For}[p][s]\).

  • \(L(T)\): one plane with any number \(m \in [1, \hat m]\) of landings, at cost \(G[m][T]\). Admissibility is ignored, so this is a relaxation. It bounds more than \(E\): under (S) alone, whatever the central flights, every feasible plan of makespan \(M\) costs at least \(L(\lfloor M/2g \rfloor)\) Lemma 3.4 machine-checked.
  • \(U(T)\): one plane with an odd number \(m\) of landings, at cost \(G[m][T]\); or a pair: two planes of even sizes \(a\) and \(b\) linked at a city \(i\), at cost \[2c_i + G[a-1][T - d_i] + G[b-1][T - d_i].\]
  • \(U_3(T)\): the gadgets of \(U\), and a chain: three planes of sizes \(a\) (even), \(b\) (odd, at least
    1. and \(b'\) (even), the first two linked at \(i\) and the last two at \(j\), at cost \[2c_i + 2c_j + G[a-1][T-d_i] + G[b-2][T-d_i-d_j] + G[b'-1][T-d_j].\]

Results.

  • \(L(T) \le E(T) \le U_3(T) \le U(T)\) Proposition 7.3 informal, parts in Lean. Each gadget of \(U\) and \(U_3\) is a link tree with one, two or three planes that satisfies (F1) and (F2), so its groups are admissible by Lemma 2.6.
  • The upper bounds are exact when useful trees are small: \(E = U\) if \(q_{\max} \le 2\), that is \(p \le 2\) or \(t \le p + 1\), and \(E = U_3\) if \(q_{\max} \le 3\) Proposition 7.4 informal. By Corollary 2.7 the trees of an optimal forest then have at most two or three planes, and such trees are a vertex, an edge or a path: exactly the gadgets.

In the example (Figure 6). \(q_{\max} = 2\), so \(E = U\) at every level. Moreover \(L = U\) at every level: all seven levels are closed by the bracket, and zeno --stats reports settled: bracket 7, pairs 0, chains 0, monotone 0, exact 0. This is no accident of the numbers. With two planes and four landings, the relaxation either splits them \(1 + 3\), two odd planes, which is always admissible since no component of \(H\) then holds an even plane Lemma 2.4 informal; or \(2 + 2\), where the cheapest choice is the same \(G\)-optimal pair twice: the two groups share a city, which makes them admissible Lemma 2.9 informal.

The contrast instance of the figure shows what an open level looks like: three cities with \(d = (3, 5, 7)\) and \(c = (10, 5, 1)\), \(t = 4\) passengers and \(p = 2\) planes (contrast.dat), so \(s = 6\) and the levels run from 9 to 21. At \(T = 12\), the cheapest relaxed split is \(\{2, 3\} \mid \{1, 1, 1, 1\}\), of cost 46: two even planes that share no city, so each component of \(H\) holds one even plane, and the split is not admissible (Lemma 2.4). The cheapest admissible split, \(\{1, 1, 2\} \mid \{1, 1, 2\}\), costs \(50 = U(12)\). Levels 12 and 14 stay open, and zeno --stats reports settled: bracket 11, pairs 2: both are settled by \(U\), exact since \(q_{\max} = 2\).

In the code. relaxedCost computes \(L\), and forestCost(r, G, T, q) runs the link-forest program of step 8 restricted to trees of at most \(q\) planes (src/zeno/forest.cpp): with \(q = 2\) and \(q = 3\) it ranges over the same trees as the gadgets of \(U\) and \(U_3\). In sweep mode, bracket() computes \(L\) and \(U\) on every level in parallel, and chains() adds \(U_3\) on the levels left open when \(q_{\max} \ge 3\). Every value is checked to lie inside its bracket.

Step 8. The link-forest DP

Idea. When \(q_{\max} \ge 4\) and \(L < U_3\) at a level, zeno computes \(E(T)\) exactly. By Lemma 2.6 and Corollary 2.7, \(E(T)\) is the least cost of \(p\) groups with \(s\) landings in all, each within \(T\), that carry a link forest whose trees have at most \(q_{\max}\) planes. Root every tree at a plane. The group of a plane is then its free landings (an odd number \(r\)), plus one landing per child edge, plus one for the edge to its parent. The program builds such trees from the leaves up, attaching to each plane the subtrees that hang from it.

Tables, for one level \(T\):

  • \(\mathrm{Ch}_q[\tau][j]\): the least cost of a multiset of child items. An item is a link label \(b\), with one landing in \(b\) in the parent’s group, and the subtree hanging from that link. The labels’ durations sum to \(\tau\), the subtrees have \(q\) planes in all, and \(j\) counts the landings;
  • \(\mathrm{Sub}_a[q][j]\): the least cost of a subtree with \(q\) planes and \(j\) landings whose root holds one landing in \(a\) for the link to its parent;
  • \(\mathrm{Root}[q][j]\): the least cost of a tree with \(q\) planes and \(j\) landings;
  • \(\mathrm{For}[P][J]\): the least cost of a forest on \(P\) planes with \(J\) landings.

For \(q = 1, \dots, q_{\max}\) in order, with \(r\) odd and \(\tau \in [0, T]\), \[\mathrm{Sub}_a[q][j] = \min_{\tau,\ j' + 1 + r = j} \big(c_a + \mathrm{Ch}_{q-1}[\tau][j'] + G[r][T - d_a - \tau]\big),\] \[\mathrm{Root}[q][j] = \min_{\tau,\ j' + r = j} \big(\mathrm{Ch}_{q-1}[\tau][j'] + G[r][T - \tau]\big),\] \[\mathrm{Ch}_q[\tau][j] = \min_{b,\ 1 \le q' \le q,\ j'} \big(\mathrm{Ch}_{q-q'}[\tau - d_b][j - 1 - j'] + c_b + \mathrm{Sub}_b[q'][j']\big),\] with \(\mathrm{Ch}_0[0][0] = 0\), and finally, with \(\mathrm{For}[0][0] = 0\), \[\mathrm{For}[P][J] = \min_{q \le \min(P, q_{\max}),\ j} \big(\mathrm{For}[P - q][J - j] + \mathrm{Root}[q][j]\big).\]

Result Theorem 7.5 machine-checked. \(\mathrm{For}[p][s] = E(T)\). Given \(G\), the program takes \(O(n\,q_{\max}^2 s^2 T + p\,q_{\max} s^2)\) time per level.

In the example, at \(T = 8\). With \(q_{\max} = 2\), a tree is a single plane or a pair.

  • A single plane with one or three landings: \(\mathrm{Root}[1][1] = G[1][8] = 1\) and \(\mathrm{Root}[1][3] = G[3][8] = 21\).
  • A leaf hanging from a link at city 2, with one free landing within \(8 - d_2 = 5\): \(\mathrm{Sub}_2[1][2] = c_2 + G[1][5] = 7\).
  • The parent’s side of that link: \(\mathrm{Ch}_1[3][3] = c_2 + \mathrm{Sub}_2[1][2] = 13\).
  • The pair, rooted at a plane with one free landing within \(8 - 3 = 5\): \[\mathrm{Root}[2][4] = \mathrm{Ch}_1[3][3] + G[1][5] = 13 + 1 = 14.\]
  • The forest, a pair or two single planes: \[\mathrm{For}[2][4] = \min\big(\mathrm{Root}[2][4],\ \mathrm{Root}[1][1] + \mathrm{Root}[1][3]\big) = \min(14, 22) = 14 = E(8).\]

The pair is the tree of Figure 2. A link at city 3 with free landings in city 2 gives the same groups and the same cost.

In the code. ForestProgram (include/zeno/forest.hpp, src/zeno/forest.cpp). Instead of dense arrays over \(\tau\), it keeps the child items of a plane as Pareto staircases over \((\tau, \text{cost})\), so its work depends on the sizes of these staircases, not on the magnitude of \(T\). reconstruct() returns an optimal LinkForest, which step 11 turns into a plan. In sweep mode the exact program runs only where the bracket and monotonicity leave a level open. Since \(E\) is non-increasing, a level between two settled levels \(a < b\) has \(E(b) \le E \le E(a)\), which tightens its bracket, and equal values at both ends settle every level between them (monotone in --stats). bisect() evaluates up to threads evenly spaced open levels exactly, and recurses on the pieces.

Step 9. The σ-DP

Idea. A second exact method works with parity matrices directly. Process the planes one at a time, choosing for each its number of landings and its row of the parity matrix, and remember only the set \(\sigma\) of cities whose column has an odd number of ones so far. At the end, \(\sigma\) must be empty, which is (h3). Some parity matrix touches few cities, so \(\sigma\) stays small.

Results.

  • For an admissible partition with \(|X| = s\), some parity matrix touches at most \(w = \min(p - 1, t - p)\) cities, and its rows have at most \(\min(|X_k| - 1, w)\) ones Lemma 2.8 machine-checked.
  • The σ-graph at level \(T\) has nodes \((k, j, \sigma)\): \(k\) planes processed, \(j\) landings used, and \(\sigma\) a set of at most \(w\) cities. An arc gives plane \(k + 1\) a size \(m\) and a row \(\eta\) with \(|\eta| \equiv m + 1 \pmod 2\), \(|\eta| \le \min(m - 1, w)\) and \(|\sigma \triangle \eta| \le w\); it leads to \((k + 1, j + m, \sigma \triangle \eta)\) and weighs \(c(\eta) + G[m - |\eta|][T - \delta(\eta)]\). The shortest path from \((0, 0, \emptyset)\) to \((p, s, \emptyset)\) weighs \(E(T)\) Theorem 7.6 machine-checked. It takes \(O(p\,s\,\hat m\,N_w)\) time per level, where \(N_w\) bounds the pairs of sets an arc can join: polynomial in \(n\) for fixed \(w\).
  • A variant carries Pareto sets of points \((c(X),\ \max_k \delta(X_k))\) along the arcs instead of one level at a time, and yields the whole front in one pass Proposition 7.8 informal.

In the example, \(w = 1\). At \(T = 8\), plane 1 takes \(m = 2\) landings with the row \(\eta = \{2\}\): an arc of weight \(c_2 + G[1][8 - 3] = 7\) from \((0, 0, \emptyset)\) to \((1, 2, \{2\})\). Plane 2 takes the same option, \(\sigma\) becomes \(\{2\} \triangle \{2\} = \emptyset\), and the path ends at \((2, 4, \emptyset)\) with weight \(14 = E(8)\).

In the code. The C++ solver uses the link-forest DP. The σ-DP belongs to the Lean tools: the verified solver zeno-front machine-checked and the Lean checker compute \(E\) with it, or with the link-forest DP when useTreeDP estimates that cheaper (Lean tools).

Step 10. Finding the breakpoints

Idea. Proposition 7.1 needs \(E\) only where it drops. Since \(E\) is non-increasing, an interval of levels whose two ends have equal values is constant and holds no breakpoint, so a search can skip it whole.

The search breakpoints (include/zeno/breakpoints.hpp). It starts from \(E(T_{\rm lo})\) and the closed form \(E(T_{\rm hi}) = s\,c_{\min}\). Every open interval \((a, b)\) with \(E(a) > E(b)\) is split at up to threads evenly spaced levels, evaluated in parallel; pieces with equal ends are dropped, and a piece \((a, a + 1)\) has its breakpoint at \(a + 1\). With \(r\) breakpoints this evaluates \(O(r \log(T_{\rm hi} - T_{\rm lo}))\) levels. Each level is evaluated by the cascade of Figure 4: \(L\) and \(U\), then \(U_3\), then the exact program, each only if the previous ones do not settle it.

--levels sweep brackets every level and runs the exact program only where needed (step 8); --levels search runs the search above; the default, auto, sweeps up to \(2^{12}\) levels and searches beyond, so on wide level ranges the number of levels evaluated grows with the logarithm of the range.

In the example. Seven levels: auto sweeps them. With --levels search --threads 1, zeno evaluates \(T = 4\), then \(7\), then \(5\) and \(8\), then \(6\) and \(9\); \(T = 10\) is the closed form. On so few levels the search saves nothing. It pays off on ranges of thousands of levels with few breakpoints.

Step 11. Plans

Construction Theorem 4.1 machine-checked. Take a link forest of the partition and root each tree. Every plane flies, in this order:

  1. its drop-offs, \(C_I \to b \to C_I\), one per child edge labelled \(b\), each leaving a new passenger in city \(b\), from time 0;
  2. its free landings as alternating crossings: \(C_I \to x \to C_G\) carrying a new passenger, then \(C_G \to x \to C_I\) empty, and so on, ending in \(C_G\); there is an odd number of them by (F2);
  3. if it has a parent edge labelled \(a\), its pick-up \(C_G \to a \to C_G\), leaving city \(a\) once the passenger its parent dropped there has arrived.

No drop-off and no crossing ever waits. A plane picks up at most once, from its parent, whose drop-offs come first; the timing argument of the theorem shows that each pick-up ends by the larger load of the two planes. So every plane flies exactly \(\ell(X_k)\) and the makespan is \(\max_k \ell(X_k)\). A forest with \(N\) edges hands off \(N\) passengers, and the crossings carry the other \(t - N\).

In the example. The plan of \((14, 16)\), as zeno --plans prints it; a leg reads from->to[depart,arrive], followed by the passenger it carries:

plane 1: CI->c2[0,3]p1 c2->CI[3,6] CI->c3[6,11]p2 c3->CG[11,16]p2
plane 2: CI->c3[0,5]p3 c3->CG[5,10]p3 CG->c2[10,13] c2->CG[13,16]p1

Plane 1 is the root: it drops passenger 1 in city 2 and returns, then crosses through city 3 with passenger 2. Plane 2 crosses through city 3 with passenger 3, flies empty from \(C_G\) to city 2, and brings back passenger 1, who has waited there since time 3. Both planes finish at \(16 = 2 \cdot 8\) (Figure 2).

Step 12. Certificates

Idea. A front can be checked without trusting the solver that found it. A certificate lists the points, a plan for each, and, for each gap between consecutive points, the name of a lower bound that the checker recomputes.

Result Theorem 9.1 machine-checked. Let \((C_1, M_1), \dots, (C_r, M_r)\) have increasing makespans and decreasing costs. The list is the front if

  1. every listed plan is feasible, with cost \(C_i\) and makespan \(M_i\): the checker simulates it;
  2. no plan is faster than \(M_1\): the checker verifies \(M_1 = 2d_{\min}\kappa\) Proposition 7.10 informal, parts in Lean;
  3. for every \(i < r\), every plan of makespan below \(M_{i+1}\) costs at least \(C_i\): a lower bound at the level \(T^\star_i = \lfloor \lfloor (M_{i+1} - 1)/2 \rfloor / g \rfloor\) reaches \(C_i\) (for a front point, \(T^\star_i = T_{i+1} - 1\));
  4. no plan is cheaper than \(C_r\): the checker verifies \(C_r = s\,c_{\min}\), since every plan lands at least \(s\) times Lemma 3.2 machine-checked.

Obligation 3 names one of two bounds:

  • relaxed: \(L(T^\star_i) \ge C_i\). Sound under (S) alone, whatever the central flights Lemma 3.4 machine-checked;
  • forest: \(E(T^\star_i) \ge C_i\), recomputed with the link-forest DP. Sound under (S) and (A1) Theorem 4.7 machine-checked, or (S) and (AΔ) Theorem 4.10 machine-checked.

zeno writes relaxed whenever \(L\) reaches the cost, so a certificate whose gaps are all relaxed holds for every table of central flights.

Non-symmetric instances

When \(d \ne \bar d\), the duration of a landing depends on how it is flown, and step 1 no longer gives the front. zeno then computes a certified bracket instead (solveBracket, include/zeno/ns.hpp).

Roles. A landing in city \(i\) is flown as a crossing \(C_I \to i \to C_G\) or back, which lasts \(d_i + \bar d_i\); as a drop-off \(C_I \to i \to C_I\), which lasts \(2d_i\); or as a pick-up \(C_G \to i \to C_G\), which lasts \(2\bar d_i\). A role configuration gives every plane its drop-offs, pick-ups and crossings, with an odd number of crossings per plane and, city by city, as many drop-offs as pick-ups. The role load \(\lambda_k\) of plane \(k\) sums the durations of its roles Definition 4.2 Lean definition.

What carries over, and what does not.

  • Under (A1-NS), central flights at least as long as \(\min(d_i + d_j,\ \bar d_i + \bar d_j)\), every plan is weakly dominated by a role configuration with the same number of passengers, no larger cost, and role loads at most its makespan Theorem 6.1 machine-checked. So the least cost of a role configuration within a load bound is a lower bound.
  • A configuration need not fly within its largest role load: a pick-up can reach a city before the passenger it collects has been dropped there, and wait. That waiting can be harmful. On an instance with two cities, the cheapest role configuration with loads at most 73 costs 14, while no plan of makespan at most 73 costs less than 18 Proposition 6.5 informal. Role loads alone do not give the front.
  • For fixed orders of the drop-offs and pick-ups and a fixed matching of drop-offs to pick-ups, the template schedule (drop-offs first, then crossings, then pick-ups, each waiting for its passenger) is optimal, and its makespan has a closed form Lemma 6.3 informal, parts in Lean. If every plane picks up at most once, or every plane drops off at most once, the template finishes within the largest role load, provided that no hub leg of a city is longer than the detour through another city and the other hub, (AΔ-path) Corollary 6.4 informal.
  • Under (A1-NS), every plan is dominated by a role configuration whose template finishes no later, without any assumption on the order of events Theorem 6.9 machine-checked. Hence the front is the Pareto set of (cost, least template makespan) over role configurations Corollary 6.10 informal, parts in Lean.

The bracket.

  1. Upper points. Plans built from link forests in which every plane picks up at most once, or, by time reversal, drops off at most once, and from the cheapest balanced role configurations found inside gaps. All are scheduled and simulated with their waiting, so every printed point is a real plan.
  2. Lower bounds per gap, for every plan of makespan below the next point:
    • relaxed: the relaxation of Lemma 3.4 machine-checked with each landing weighted \(\min(2d_i, 2\bar d_i)\), which needs no assumption on central flights;
    • roles: the role relaxation, in which each plane flies an odd number of crossings and the loads add up to the crossing weight of the landings (Theorem 6.1, so (A1-NS));
    • lagrangian: a Lagrangian relaxation of the per-city balance, with one multiplier per city (A1-NS);
    • balanced: the exact program over balanced role configurations, a shortest path over the planes on the vector of per-city imbalances Proposition 6.6 informal, parts in Lean. It is exponential in \(t - p\) and stops at the roleStates and roleWork limits;
    • search: the exhaustive search over the role configurations cheaper than the gap’s cost, each with every drop-off order and matching, the closed form of Lemma 6.3 giving its makespan. It rests on Theorem 6.9 and Corollary 6.10, stops at searchWork, and is the only kind that closes gaps opened by harmful waiting.
  3. Report. The front is exact when the fastest makespan is certified and every gap is closed. Otherwise zeno lists each open gap with its proved lower bound and the reason it stays open, rather than print a front it cannot prove (command line). The refinement of open gaps stops after refineSeconds.

On 1,800 random tiny non-symmetric instances checked against a brute-force oracle, every bracket closes and equals the oracle’s front (README.md).

Complexity

From §8 of the theory note, with \(\hat m = s - p + 1\), \(q_{\max} = \min(p, t - p + 1)\), \(w = \min(p - 1, t - p)\) and \(N_w\) as in step 9:

Step Time
One-plane table \(G\), all levels \(O(n\,\hat m\,T_{\rm hi})\)
\(L\) and \(U\), per level \(O(p\,s^2 + n\,\hat m^2)\)
\(U_3\) chains, per level \(O(n^2\,\hat m\,s + p\,s^2)\)
Link-forest DP, per level (Theorem 7.5) \(O(n\,q_{\max}^2 s^2 T + p\,q_{\max} s^2)\)
σ-DP, per level (Theorem 7.6) \(O(p\,s\,\hat m\,N_w)\)
Plan for one point \(O(s + n + p)\), up to union–find

How hard the problem is.

  • Deciding whether a fixed multiset \(X\) has an admissible partition with every \(\delta(X_k) \le T\), that is \(M(X) \le 2T\), is strongly NP-complete Theorem 8.1 informal, by a reduction from 3-Partition. So a method that fixes the multiset first and then schedules it, for instance greedily with the longest landing first onto the least-loaded plane, cannot be exact in general unless P = NP, and that greedy does miss front points (§5) informal, parts in Lean. The link-forest DP never fixes the multiset: it chooses the landings and their split together.
  • With two planes the decision stays NP-complete; for every fixed \(p\) it takes time polynomial in \(n\), \(s\) and \(T\), so it is weakly NP-complete Proposition 8.2 informal.
  • \(E(T)\), and under (S) and (A1) the front, are computable in pseudo-polynomial time (Theorem 7.5), so computing the front is not strongly NP-hard unless P = NP Corollary 8.3 informal. Whether deciding \(E(T) \le C\) is NP-hard, with \(t\) written in unary, is open.

The bounds of the table are those of the dense algorithm. The staircase layouts make the table and the forest DP independent of the magnitude of the durations in practice, and the breakpoint search evaluates \(O(r \log(T_{\rm hi} - T_{\rm lo}))\) levels instead of all of them. Measured scaling is on the results and size guidance pages.

Reproducing this page

With build/release/zeno built (installation), from the repository root:

python3 site/gen_algorithm.py

The script needs nothing else. For the worked example and the contrast instance, it recomputes from their definitions the table \(G\), and at every level \(L\), \(U\), \(U_3\), and \(E\) three ways: by the link-forest DP, by the σ-DP, and by brute force over every partition of every multiset of \(s\) landings, with the parity matrix of Definition 2.2 deciding admissibility. It checks that these agree with one another and with zeno: the breakpoints of \(E\) are the front zeno prints, the levels the bracket closes are those --stats counts, the gap kinds are those of the certificate zeno writes, and every number this page quotes still holds. It then writes the data of the figures and the tables into site/algorithm/. The three drawings are hand-made SVG files in the same directory.
