The problem

MultiZenoTravel: fly every passenger from the start hub to the goal hub, trading the total landing cost against the makespan.

MultiZenoTravel is a planning problem with two objectives. Planes carry passengers from a start hub to a goal hub through intermediate cities. Each landing in an intermediate city has a price, and the cheap cities are the far ones. A plan is judged on its cost, the sum of its landing prices, and on its makespan, the time at which the last plane reaches the goal. No plan is best on both counts, so the answer is a Pareto front: every (cost, makespan) pair that some plan achieves and that no other plan matches or beats on both. ZenoSolver computes it exactly.

The model

  • Cities. Two hubs, \(C_I\) where everything starts and \(C_G\) where everything must end, and \(n\) central cities \(1, \dots, n\).
  • Flights. Central city \(i\) is joined to \(C_I\) by a flight of duration \(d_i\) and to \(C_G\) by a flight of duration \(\bar d_i\); flights can be flown both ways. No flight joins \(C_I\) and \(C_G\), so every trip lands in a central city. Two central cities may be joined. In the instances zeno generates by default, as in the example, such a flight is never faster than going through a hub (assumption (A1)); in metric instances every flight lasts the distance between its ends, so the durations satisfy the triangle inequality instead (assumption (AΔ), §1.4 Lean definition). The instance is symmetric when \(\bar d_i = d_i\) for every city.
  • Cost. Landing in central city \(i\) costs \(c_i\). Landing in a hub is free.
  • Planes. \(p\) planes start in \(C_I\) at time 0 and must end in \(C_G\). A plane carries at most one passenger at a time.
  • Passengers. \(t \ge p\) passengers start in \(C_I\) and must reach \(C_G\). A passenger may leave a plane in a central city and board another one there later: a hand-off. Planes and passengers may wait anywhere.
  • Objectives. The cost of a plan is the sum of the landing costs of all its flights. Its makespan is the time at which the last plane arrives in \(C_G\). The front is the set of (cost, makespan) pairs of the plans that no other plan dominates.

The precise definitions are §1.1 Lean definition (instances), §1.2 Lean definition (plans) and §1.5 Lean definition (fronts) of the blueprint. One consequence is used everywhere: a plan lands at least \(s = 2t - p\) times in central cities (Lemma 3.2 machine-checked).

An example

Front, as (cost, makespan): (4, 20), (14, 16), (20, 14), (24, 12), (30, 10), (36, 8).

City 1 is close to both hubs and expensive; city 3 is far and cheap. With \(t = 3\) passengers and \(p = 2\) planes, a plan lands at least \(2t - p = 4\) times. Four landings in city 3 cost \(4 \times 1 = 4\), but each plane then flies four legs of 5: makespan 20. Four landings in city 1 cost 36 and take 8. The rest of the front lies in between. The command

build/release/zeno -n 3 -t 3 -p 2 -d site/problem/example.dat --plans

prints the front and, for each point, a plan that zeno has checked by simulation: one line per plane, each leg written from->to[departure,arrival], followed by pK when it carries passenger K.

Cost and makespan

The cost counts every landing in a central city, whichever plane makes it; hub landings are free. The makespan is the last arrival in \(C_G\): delivering a passenger early does not shorten it.

In a symmetric instance, a plane whose landings form the multiset \(X_k\) can fly them in exactly \(\ell(X_k) = 2\sum_{i \in X_k} d_i\), one round trip from a hub per landing, and a plan exists whose makespan is the largest of these loads (Theorem 4.1 machine-checked). No plan does better (Theorem 4.7 machine-checked), so the front is a statement about multisets of landings and how they split among the planes (Corollary 4.8 machine-checked).

The whole front

Moving along the front buys time with money. The cheapest plan, (4, 20), makes all four landings in city 3. The fastest, (36, 8), makes all four in city 1. In between, the planes mix cities: at (20, 14) each plane lands once in city 1 and once in city 3, flies \(2 \times (2 + 5) = 14\), and the four landings cost \(9 + 1 + 9 + 1 = 20\). Why it is hard

Hand-offs

Passengers can change planes, so a plan is not a set of independent trips. Without a hand-off, a plane that carries two passengers must come back to \(C_I\), which takes at least three landings; in the example no such plan ends before 12. With one hand-off each plane lands twice, and the plan ends at 8. The search therefore ranges over who drops whom where, and who collects them, not only over which cities to use.

Parity: not every split can be flown

Between two visits to a hub, a plane flies a segment. A crossing \(A\) carries a passenger from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\), a return \(\bar A\) flies back, a drop-off \(B\) leaves a passenger in a central city, and a pick-up \(\bar B\) collects one. A route from \(C_I\) to \(C_G\) has one more crossing than returns (Lemma 2.1 informal, parts in Lean), so a plane that only crosses lands an odd number of times. A plane with an even number of landings must drop off or pick up, and a drop-off in city \(i\) needs another plane’s pick-up in city \(i\).

So a split of the landings among the planes can be flown without waiting only if these parities can be matched. Definition 2.2 Lean definition (admissible partition) states the condition, and Lemma 2.6 machine-checked turns it into a forest of hand-offs between planes. In the example, the split \(\{1, 3\} \mid \{1, 3\}\) is admissible: both planes land twice, and they share city 1, where the hand-off happens. The split \(\{1, 1\} \mid \{3, 3\}\) has the same number of landings but cannot be flown: each plane would drop a passenger that the other plane never visits.

Splitting the landings among the planes

Even for a fixed multiset of landings, the best split is a scheduling problem, and the natural greedy rule, longest landing first onto the least-loaded plane, is not exact. On the instance lpt-counterexample of the catalogue (two cities, \(d = (6, 10)\), \(c = (40, 20)\), \(t = 5\), \(p = 2\)), three landings in the \(d = 10\) city and five in the \(d = 6\) city split as \(\{10, 10, 10\} \mid \{6, 6, 6, 6, 6\}\): both planes fly 60, which gives the front point (260, 60). Longest first ends at 64, a makespan that the cheaper point (240, 64) already reaches (§5 informal, parts in Lean). Deciding whether a multiset can be flown within a given makespan is strongly NP-complete (Theorem 8.1 informal), so no polynomial rule of this kind is exact unless P = NP.

Waiting can cost time

In a symmetric instance, waiting never costs time: the plans of Theorem 4.1 machine-checked end at the largest flight time of a plane. When the two hubs are at different distances (\(\bar d_i \ne d_i\)), a plane can reach a city before the passenger it must collect. Proposition 6.5 informal gives an instance with two cities, \(d = (3, 19)\), \(\bar d = (3, 11)\), \(c = (5, 1)\), \(t = 7\) and \(p = 4\). At cost 14 the flight times of the planes fit within 72, but the plane that collects three passengers in city 2 gets there at time 17, before the first drop-off can arrive at 19. It waits, and the plan ends at 74, which is the front point of cost 14. In this non-symmetric case zeno brackets the front between plans and lower bounds, and says on its standard error whether the two meet.
